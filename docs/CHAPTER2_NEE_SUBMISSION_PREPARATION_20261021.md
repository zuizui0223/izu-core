## Short scope enquiry (draft)

Dear Editors,

Would a modelling Article entitled “Repeatable benefits of selfing and variable paths of floral evolution in an island plant model” be of interest to Nature Ecology & Evolution?

Self-compatible plants are common on islands, yet comparisons with mainland relatives yield no uniform reduction in flower size. Why is reproductive assurance repeatedly realized while selection on investment in attraction yields variable outcomes? In a finite diploid model, autonomous selfing fills ovules missed by outcross pollen. This reduces the maternal seed gain from an additional visit, while the visit can still carry pollen to another plant.

Deterministic genotype densities predicted both traits changing, assurance first, in two low-service settings. Few finite populations reproduced that sequence, and some changed assurance alone. In a separate offspring census, even an allele expected to increase could leave no copy. Investment declined even with assurance fixed. Allowing assurance to evolve lowered investment on the higher-service side and narrowed the gap in three of four fresh-founder settings. With a persistent visitor the gap widened at both resource budgets. In a paired pilot the native individual return increased assurance-first sequences from 1/32 to 17/32, while the other 15 native populations crossed only the assurance threshold. In a separate registered experiment, assurance evolution improved persistence under harsh resources. Where every population survived, it increased the loss of alleles needed to regain one founder-relative investment level. These comparisons make two field predictions: similar displays may conceal differences in pollination service, and surviving populations with similar displays may differ in their capacity for future floral change. Common-grown families, pollen supplementation and paternity estimates could distinguish inherited allocation from the maternal and paternal returns to visits.

The manuscript uses five Results sections and six main figures. Its abstract follows below.

Why does reproductive assurance recur under pollen limitation while floral investment varies? In a finite diploid island plant model, delayed selfing filled ovules missed by outcross pollen. Assigning the seed benefit to the individual expressing assurance produced assurance-first sequences in 17/32 populations versus 1/32 when filling probabilities were pooled. In the other 15 native populations only assurance crossed the response threshold. Attraction trades maternal pollen receipt and paternal export against resources available for ovules. Investment declined with assurance fixed. Evolving assurance narrowed investment differences in three fresh-founder settings but widened them at both resource budgets with a persistent visitor. Exploratory matched-history analyses across twelve model conditions found more repeatable assurance endpoints. Visitor histories reduced prediction error for matching and investment by 40.15% versus 0.36%; these differences do not isolate stochastic drift. Registered experiments showed increased loss of alleles supporting one founder-relative investment target by 8.33 percentage points where all populations survived. Under harsher resources assurance improved survival, yielding a 92.71-point policy-by-resource interaction. Thus population persistence does not guarantee genetic options for future floral change.

## Formal cover letter (draft)

Dear Editors,

Please consider “Repeatable benefits of selfing and variable paths of floral evolution in an island plant model” for publication as an Article in Nature Ecology & Evolution.

Island colonists face both an immediate reproductive challenge and a subsequent evolutionary one: successful reproduction need not preserve the variation needed for a later floral response. We use a finite diploid model to connect those outcomes while separating reproductive assurance, investment in attraction and visitor matching. The study combines intervention comparisons, registered demographic and genetic endpoints, and explicitly exploratory analyses of predictability and transfer.

Our central question is how an initially predictable benefit changes the conditions for later evolution. Earlier assurance expression shifted recruitment toward selfing. In a separate cohort, finite transmission sometimes eliminated a lower-investment allele expected to increase. These comparisons identify distinct steps through separate interventions. Investment decline does not require assurance evolution, yet evolving assurance changes environmental investment contrasts. Assurance also changes persistence and retention of a specified floral option under distinct resource conditions. This connects reproductive ecology to evolutionary predictability without equating low predictive skill with drift, or target-allele retention with general evolvability. The island setting supplies testable ecological questions; speciation and mainland recolonization remain implications for future work. A related analysis of contemporary island floras is in preparation in a separate repository; it is neither a published citation nor direct empirical validation of the present model, and any concurrent submission will be disclosed accurately.

All findings derive from existing experiments and saved outputs. The manuscript retains null and inconclusive outcomes, separates registered from exploratory results, and provides source-linked figures and reproducibility records.
