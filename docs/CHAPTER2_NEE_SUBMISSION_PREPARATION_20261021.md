## Short scope enquiry (draft)

Dear Editors,

We are preparing a modelling Article entitled “Reproductive assurance can evolve first while floral investment varies in an island plant model” for Nature Ecology & Evolution.

An island plant that can fill unfertilized ovules by selfing gains seeds even before its flowers change. Does that immediate benefit also predict how much the plant will invest in attracting visitors? This question matters for the interpretation of island floras: self-compatible species are overrepresented, yet island relatives do not consistently have smaller flowers.

We varied who receives the seed benefit of selfing in a finite diploid model. Linking that benefit to each plant's own assurance increased the number of populations in which assurance rose before investment fell from one to 17 out of 32. In the remaining 15 populations with individual returns, assurance rose without a sustained investment decline. A visit can still sire offspring elsewhere, and alleles favoured in the parents can be absent from their offspring. The pilot establishes the effect on order; it leaves the contributions of those two processes in individual populations open.

The contrast across environments also changed sign. Evolving assurance narrowed the investment gap in three of four fresh-founder settings but widened it at both resource budgets in a separate experiment pairing the same founders across visitor activities. We propose that the breeding system and floral display of island plants can record different aspects of their pollination history. A field test would compare inherited allocation in common-grown families and measure autonomous seed production and outcross paternity in their source populations. Could this model-based account of the island syndrome be of interest to your readers?

The manuscript uses five Results sections and six main figures. Its abstract follows below.

Island syndromes often pair selfing with reduced floral display. We asked whether selection for autonomous seed production also predicts declining investment in attraction. In a finite diploid model, delayed selfing filled ovules missed by outcross pollen. When plants gained seeds according to their own assurance, assurance rose before investment fell in 17/32 populations, compared with 1/32 when filling probabilities were pooled at the same expected seed total for a given parental state. In the other 15 populations with individual returns, only assurance crossed the sustained response threshold. Attraction still affected pollen receipt and export and cost resources otherwise available for ovules. Favourable alleles could disappear during finite transmission. Investment could decline with assurance fixed. Evolving assurance narrowed investment differences between visitor environments in three of four fresh-founder settings but widened them at both resource budgets with a persistent visitor. Registered experiments showed improved survival under harsh resources and greater loss of alleles supporting ancestral investment where all populations survived. Thus a common benefit of selfing can recur across island populations with different floral trajectories.

## Formal cover letter (draft)

Dear Editors,

Please consider “Reproductive assurance can evolve first while floral investment varies in an island plant model” for publication as an Article in Nature Ecology & Evolution.

Island colonists face both an immediate reproductive challenge and a subsequent evolutionary one: successful reproduction need not preserve the variation needed for a later floral response. We use a finite diploid model to connect those outcomes while separating reproductive assurance, investment in attraction and visitor matching. The study combines intervention comparisons, registered demographic and genetic endpoints, and explicitly exploratory analyses of predictability and transfer.

Our central question is how an initially predictable benefit changes the conditions for later evolution. Earlier assurance expression shifted recruitment toward selfing. In a separate cohort, finite transmission sometimes eliminated a lower-investment allele expected to increase. These comparisons identify distinct steps through separate interventions. Investment decline does not require assurance evolution, yet evolving assurance changes environmental investment contrasts. Assurance also changes persistence and retention of a specified floral option under distinct resource conditions. This connects reproductive ecology to evolutionary predictability without equating low predictive skill with drift, or target-allele retention with general evolvability. The island setting supplies testable ecological questions; speciation and mainland recolonization remain implications for future work. A related analysis of contemporary island floras is in preparation in a separate repository; it is neither a published citation nor direct empirical validation of the present model, and any concurrent submission will be disclosed accurately.

All findings derive from existing experiments and saved outputs. The manuscript retains null and inconclusive outcomes, separates registered from exploratory results, and provides source-linked figures and reproducibility records.
