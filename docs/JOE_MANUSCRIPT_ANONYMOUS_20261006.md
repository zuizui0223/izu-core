# Reproductive assurance can precede floral investment decline without causing it under pollinator limitation

## Abstract

1. Reduced pollinator service is often associated with greater reproductive assurance and reduced investment in pollinator attraction. When assurance changes first, this sequence can be interpreted as evidence that increased selfing subsequently relaxes selection on attraction. Temporal precedence, however, does not establish causal necessity.

2. We tested this distinction in an explicit plant–pollinator eco-evolutionary model in which visitor functional types determine finite pollen transfer and plants reproduce through outcrossing and prior or delayed selfing. We separated three questions: whether visitor limitation changes the reproductive return to floral investment before plant evolution; which trait changes first under sustained limitation; and whether investment still declines when reproductive-assurance capacity is prevented from evolving.

3. In the delayed-selfing, assurance-cost 0.5 setting, stronger visitor limitation reversed the marginal reproductive contribution of floral investment at the same plant state from +0.5793 to −0.7004 as the outcross component fell from +1.6523 to +0.0854. In a prospectively frozen independent replication using 64 new visitor histories and eight nested demographic repeats per history, reproductive assurance crossed a declared sustained-change threshold first in 51/64 histories (proportion 0.797; 95% history-bootstrap 0.688–0.891).

4. A separate preregistered intervention held assurance capacity fixed at 0.5. Investment still declined under stronger visitor limitation: mean far change −0.3060 [−0.3181, −0.2941] and far-minus-near contrast −0.4354 [−0.4533, −0.4172]. The assurance-first sequence was not universal: under prior selfing with positive mutation, only 30/64 histories were assurance-first at the same threshold.

5. **Synthesis.** Pollinator limitation can generate an assurance-first evolutionary sequence without making assurance evolution necessary for subsequent decline in attraction investment. Ecological change can act directly on the reproductive return to attraction, so the order in which traits evolve should not be treated as a causal pathway without intervention-based tests.

## Keywords

floral evolution; island ecology; mating system; plant–pollinator interactions; pollen limitation; reproductive assurance; selfing syndrome

# Introduction

Pollinator limitation can affect plant evolution through more than one biological route. Reduced or altered visitation changes pollen delivery and mating opportunities, which can favour reproductive assurance when outcrossing fails. The same ecological change can also alter the return on floral traits that attract or match pollinators. Because these processes occur within the same reproductive system, a sequence in which increased selfing or reproductive assurance is observed before floral reduction is tempting to read as a serial mechanism: assurance evolves first, then reduced dependence on pollinators weakens selection on attraction.

That interpretation is biologically plausible, but temporal order alone cannot establish it. A trait can change first because it responds faster, has more accessible genetic variation, or has a larger immediate selection gradient. A second trait can then change later for an independent reason generated by the same environmental shift. Distinguishing these possibilities requires separating three objects that are often conflated: the ecological return on a trait before evolution, the temporal order of realized evolutionary change, and the causal necessity of one evolving trait for the other response.

Pollination-mediated evolution on islands provides a useful setting for this problem. Island isolation can reduce the replenishment or functional diversity of pollinators and has repeatedly been linked to changes in plant mating systems, floral morphology and pollination function. Global and comparative work shows that island pollination networks and mating systems often differ from mainland systems, but floral responses are not uniform among lineages or archipelagos (Traveset et al., 2016; Grossenbacher et al., 2017; Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson, 2020). Functional changes in island pollinator communities can also alter trait matching and pollination performance (Hiraiwa & Ushimaru, 2017, 2024). These patterns make islands a natural context in which to ask whether a repeated ecological pressure should be expected to produce one sequential floral syndrome.

Experimental evolution already shows that pollinator loss can rapidly increase autonomous selfing. In *Mimulus guttatus*, populations evolving without bumblebees increased autonomous self-fertilization, and a sequential selfing-syndrome interpretation was proposed in which traits affecting selfing efficiency can respond before traits such as flower size (Bodbyl Roels & Kelly, 2011). Subsequent work showed that pollinator loss can also reduce genome-wide genetic variation (Busch et al., 2022). Pollinator identity can drive divergent evolution of both floral signals and mating-system traits over only a few generations (Gervasi & Schiestl, 2017). Theory likewise predicts joint evolution of selfing and investment in attractive structures (Sakai, 1995). The novelty question is therefore not whether reproductive assurance can evolve before or alongside floral change. It is whether an observed assurance-first sequence demonstrates that assurance evolution is required for later floral reduction.

We address that question with one explicit plant–pollinator model. Plants carry inherited traits for functional matching, pollinator-facing floral investment and reproductive assurance. Visitor functional types determine finite compatible pollen transfer; maternal and paternal outcross reproduction, selfing, inbreeding depression, inheritance and finite recruitment determine which trait values persist. Continued visitor establishment and disappearance generate time-varying pollinator environments. The model is mechanistic but synthetic: visitor distance, reproductive updates and trait axes are not calibrated to kilometres, years or named island phenotypes.

Our inference is organized around three linked contrasts. First, fixed-plant assays ask whether visitor limitation changes the marginal reproductive return to floral investment before any plant trait evolves. Second, sustained-isolation trajectories ask whether reproductive assurance or floral investment reaches a declared evolutionary-change threshold first. Third, a matched intervention prevents assurance capacity from evolving and tests whether investment still declines. This design directly separates sequence from causal necessity.

We made one primary claim before the independent confirmation was run: under delayed selfing with assurance cost 0.5 and positive mutation, reproductive assurance can change before floral investment declines, but assurance evolution is not required for the investment decline. We did not preregister a universal sequence across reproductive settings. The confirmatory design therefore reports alternative settings as scope tests rather than using them to rescue or broaden the primary result.

A final reproductive assay addresses a related interpretive issue. Pollination studies often summarize pollen limitation as a fractional deficit, whereas evolution acts through absolute viable reproductive contribution after allocation costs, selfing and inbreeding depression. These quantities need not move together. We therefore ask whether an intervention that slightly reduces a viable pollen deficit necessarily increases viable offspring production.

# Materials and Methods

## Model overview

The model represents a focal diploid plant population embedded in an externally supported visitor environment. Plants carry additive loci for three abstract traits: functional matching or access, pollinator-facing floral investment, and reproductive-assurance capacity. Trait values lie on bounded synthetic axes and are not calibrated to flower colour, corolla size, nectar guides or a particular island flora.

Visitor functional types differ in their position on the matching axis. At each reproductive update, the current visitor assemblage determines compatible pollen transfer. Floral investment affects pollinator-mediated reproductive contribution but carries an allocation cost. Outcrossing contributes both maternal and paternal reproduction. Depending on the declared reproductive setting, autonomous selfing occurs prior to or after opportunities for outcrossing. Viability of selfed offspring is reduced by fixed inbreeding depression. Mendelian inheritance and mutation generate offspring genotypes, after which finite recruitment and density regulation determine the next plant population.

Visitor communities are generated by continuing establishment and disappearance of functional types. The manipulated isolation coordinate changes successful visitor establishment while the per-type disappearance process is retained. The focal comparison uses a higher-replenishment “near” environment and a more strongly supply-limited “far” environment. These labels describe model treatments only; they are not calibrated geographic distances.

The primary plant capacity is 48 and there is no plant immigration in the maintained-isolation experiments. Time is measured in reproductive updates rather than calendar years.

## Reproductive settings and primary scope

Two reproductive settings are retained in the paper. The preregistered primary cell uses delayed selfing, assurance cost 0.5 and mutation probability 0.01. A prior-selfing setting with zero assurance cost provides a scope comparison. These settings jointly differ in selfing timing and assurance cost and therefore cannot be interpreted as a clean one-factor test of timing.

The primary claim is explicitly restricted to the delayed-selfing, costly-assurance, positive-mutation cell. Other setting-by-mutation combinations are reported to identify where the temporal sequence does or does not generalize.

## Fixed-plant reproductive-return assay

To identify the ecological change upstream of evolution, we held plant state fixed and compared near and far visitor exposures. Assurance capacity was fixed at 0.5. The assay decomposes the local contribution of floral investment into outcross, viable-selfed and total reproductive components at the same plant phenotype. Because plant traits do not change in this calculation, any near–far difference in the marginal contribution of investment is generated by the visitor environment rather than by prior plant evolution.

The focal result uses visitor snapshot 400 in the delayed-selfing setting. The numerical derivative is a local contribution diagnostic, not an evolutionary velocity and not an estimate of a natural selection coefficient.

## Sustained-isolation evolutionary experiment

The discovery cohort used 64 independent visitor histories and eight nested demographic repeats per history. Near and far arms experienced different visitor replenishment throughout 1,000 reproductive updates. Three plant traits evolved jointly. The independent visitor history, not the demographic repeat, is the ecological replication unit.

Temporal order was evaluated on history-level mean trajectories. A trait was considered to have changed when its founder-relative displacement reached 0.05 in the predicted direction and remained beyond that threshold for 20 consecutive updates. Crossings within five updates were classified as near-simultaneous. Thresholds 0.025 and 0.10 were retained as sensitivity analyses. A threshold crossing is a declared diagnostic of realized change, not the onset of an infinitesimal evolutionary response.

The discovery analysis found an assurance-first pattern in the delayed/costly positive-mutation cell. Because this temporal readout was not originally confirmatory, we subsequently froze an independent replication before generating any new outcomes.

## Prospectively frozen independent confirmation

The confirmatory campaign used 64 new visitor-history seeds and eight new nested demographic-repeat seeds that did not overlap the discovery cohort. The complete campaign contained 4,096 trajectories. The design, seed ranges, thresholds, tie rule, bootstrap seeds, success rules and no-retuning rule were frozen before outcome generation.

For the temporal-order component, the primary cell was delayed selfing, assurance cost 0.5 and mutation probability 0.01. The success criterion required two conditions across all 64 independent histories: the assurance-first proportion had to exceed 0.50, and the lower bound of its 95% visitor-history bootstrap interval had to exceed 0.50. Near-simultaneous, investment-first and censored histories therefore counted as not assurance-first for this binary primary proportion.

All four setting-by-mutation temporal cells were reported regardless of direction. Secondary cells could not rescue or overturn the primary adjudication.

## Fixed-assurance necessity intervention

A separate confirmatory intervention tested whether assurance evolution was necessary for investment decline. All plants began with assurance capacity 0.5, and assurance was held fixed at that value while matching and investment retained their inherited dynamics. Fixed assurance is not equivalent to zero realized selfing: selfing opportunities can still change with pollen supply. The experiment therefore tests necessity of *assurance evolution*, not necessity of selfing itself and not a complete mediation fraction.

The preregistered primary estimands at update 1,000 were (i) far investment change from the matched founder mean and (ii) the far-minus-near investment contrast. Confirmation required both means to be negative and both 95% visitor-history bootstrap upper bounds to be below zero. Each near and far arm also had to retain at least 90% occupancy, with at least 60 of 64 histories estimable; otherwise the result would be declared inconclusive.

## Pollen-deficit and viable-offspring assay

A fixed-trait intervention varied investment while holding assurance capacity and visitor exposure constant. We compared the fractional viable pollen deficit with absolute viable maternal offspring. The focal delayed/far comparison increases investment from 0.25 to 0.75 at assurance capacity 0.5. This is a reproductive accounting experiment, not an evolutionary trajectory.

## Statistical reporting and reproducibility

All uncertainty for the primary confirmation is based on visitor-history resampling. Demographic repeats are nested within histories and do not increase the independent ecological denominator beyond 64. Trait endpoints after extinction are undefined rather than coded as zero.

The confirmatory design prohibited outcome-dependent seed extension, threshold changes, tie-window changes, outcome substitution and biological parameter retuning. All 4,096 declared trajectories and hash-verified receipts were required before the biological readout was produced. The primary history-level confirmation data and complete confirmatory result are retained in the repository. The unresolved high-resolution positive-mutation deterministic/diffusion comparison is not used as evidence for the biological claim.

# Results

## Visitor limitation lowers the return on floral investment before plant evolution

At the same fixed plant state, the reproductive return to floral investment differed sharply between visitor environments. In the delayed-selfing setting at snapshot 400, the mean total investment contribution derivative was +0.5793 under near exposure but −0.7004 under far exposure. The outcross component declined from +1.6523 to +0.0854. The viable-selfed component changed in the opposite direction and partly offset the decline in outcross return rather than generating it.

The intrinsic investment-cost coefficient was unchanged across the comparison. Thus stronger visitor limitation was sufficient to make additional pollinator-facing investment locally unfavourable before assurance or investment itself evolved.

## Reproductive assurance precedes investment decline in the primary setting

In the discovery cohort, reproductive assurance reached the declared 0.05 sustained-change threshold before investment in 51/64 far-history means in the delayed-selfing, costly-assurance, positive-mutation setting; the remaining 13 histories were classified as near-simultaneous.

The prospectively frozen independent replication reproduced the same counts with entirely new visitor histories and demographic repeats: 51/64 histories were assurance-first and 13/64 were near-simultaneous. The assurance-first proportion was 0.796875, with a 95% visitor-history bootstrap interval of 0.6875–0.890625. Both components of the frozen success rule were therefore satisfied.

The result was robust to the two predeclared temporal thresholds within the primary setting. At a 0.025 threshold, 48/64 histories were assurance-first and the 95% interval was 0.640625–0.844531. At 0.10, 59/64 were assurance-first with interval 0.84375–0.984375. The result therefore does not depend on the exact choice among these three declared thresholds.

However, the temporal pattern was not universal. In the independent prior-selfing positive-mutation cell at the primary 0.05 threshold, only 30/64 histories were assurance-first and 34/64 were near-simultaneous. The 95% interval for the assurance-first proportion was 0.34375–0.59375. We therefore retain the assurance-first claim only for the delayed-selfing, costly-assurance setting.

## Assurance evolution is not necessary for investment decline

Preventing assurance capacity from evolving did not prevent investment decline. In the independent confirmatory intervention, all 64 delayed/costly histories remained estimable and terminal occupancy was 1.0 in both near and far arms.

With assurance fixed at 0.5, mean far investment change from the founder state was −0.306021, with a 95% visitor-history bootstrap interval of −0.318054 to −0.294111. The far-minus-near investment contrast was −0.435389, with interval −0.453330 to −0.417191. Both preregistered means were negative and both upper interval bounds remained below zero, so the fixed-assurance confirmation criterion passed.

The secondary prior-selfing cell showed the same sign for the necessity intervention: far investment change was −0.331600 [−0.341782, −0.320989] and far-minus-near investment was −0.309376 [−0.324630, −0.294177]. This broader sign pattern does not broaden the temporal-order claim, which failed to show a majority assurance-first pattern in the corresponding positive-mutation prior-selfing cell.

Together, the temporal and intervention experiments separate sequence from causal requirement. Assurance can change first in the primary setting, but the later decline in investment does not require assurance capacity itself to evolve.

## A smaller pollen deficit can coincide with fewer viable offspring

The reproductive accounting assay showed that pollen-deficit metrics and absolute viable reproduction can move in different directions. In the delayed/far snapshot-400 comparison, increasing investment from 0.25 to 0.75 at assurance capacity 0.5 reduced the fractional viable pollen deficit by 0.0104 but reduced viable maternal offspring by 15.72 per 48 plants.

Thus an intervention can improve a fractional pollen-supply metric while decreasing absolute viable reproductive output once allocation costs, mating route and inbreeding depression are retained.

# Discussion

## Temporal precedence is not causal necessity

The central result is a separation between evolutionary order and evolutionary necessity. Under the declared delayed-selfing, costly-assurance conditions, reproductive assurance repeatedly changed before floral investment declined. That result was independently reproduced under a prospectively frozen design. Yet a separate intervention showed that investment still declined strongly when assurance capacity was prevented from evolving.

The simplest interpretation is not that the assurance-first sequence is spurious. Rather, the sequence and the investment decline share an upstream ecological cause without forming a single obligatory serial pathway. Visitor limitation creates unfilled outcrossing opportunities that can favour reproductive assurance, while simultaneously reducing the reproductive return to additional pollinator-facing investment. These two selective consequences can have different temporal dynamics.

The fixed-plant assay identifies this upstream route directly. Before any plant trait evolved, the far visitor environment nearly eliminated the outcross return to additional investment and reversed the total local contribution of investment. The selfed component partly offset this change rather than causing it. Assurance evolution can subsequently modify the reproductive context, but it is not required to initiate the decline in investment under the tested conditions.

This distinction matters because evolutionary sequences are often narratively converted into causal chains. A trait that changes earlier is a candidate mediator, but temporal precedence can also reflect faster response, accessible standing variation, a steeper gradient or different threshold-crossing geometry. Intervention is required to determine whether the earlier trait change is necessary for the later response.

## Relation to the selfing syndrome

An assurance-first sequence is not itself new. Experimental pollinator loss in *Mimulus guttatus* produced rapid evolution of autonomous selfing and motivated a sequential view of selfing-syndrome evolution (Bodbyl Roels & Kelly, 2011). Pollinator identity can also drive rapid joint evolution of mating-system and attraction traits (Gervasi & Schiestl, 2017), and theoretical models have long coupled selfing with allocation to attractive structures (Sakai, 1995). Pollinator loss can additionally reduce genome-wide genetic variation (Busch et al., 2022).

Our result therefore does not claim that reproductive assurance is generally first, nor that joint floral and mating-system evolution is novel. The contribution is the explicit separation of three stages within one reproductive process: ecological return before plant evolution, observed evolutionary sequence, and causal necessity tested by intervention.

The scope boundary is biologically informative. The prior-selfing positive-mutation setting did not reproduce an assurance-first majority at the primary threshold. This prevents a universal sequence interpretation and shows that reproductive assumptions alter the timing of realized multivariate response. The supported generalization is structural rather than universal: temporal order and causal dependence are distinct properties and should be tested separately.

## Island isolation as limitation of visitor replenishment

The model connects to island ecology through a specific mechanism: continued visitor replenishment. Stronger isolation reduces successful establishment of visitor functional types while disappearance continues. This can create long periods of low effective visitor availability and change the reproductive value of pollinator-facing traits.

We deliberately do not equate the model axis with geographic kilometres or island area. Geographic isolation in nature can affect pollinator arrival, seed immigration, population size, community composition and disturbance simultaneously. The present model isolates one of those channels so that its consequences can be interpreted cleanly.

This makes the model most useful as a hypothesis about process rather than as a reconstruction of a named archipelago. Comparative studies show that island plants often differ in self-compatibility, floral traits and pollination networks, but the direction and magnitude of floral change are heterogeneous among systems (Traveset et al., 2016; Grossenbacher et al., 2017; Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson, 2020). Functional changes in island pollinator communities can alter trait matching and pollination function (Hiraiwa & Ushimaru, 2017, 2024). Our model offers one reason repeated pollinator limitation need not produce one obligate trait sequence: assurance and attraction can respond to the same ecological shift through partly parallel reproductive routes.

## Pollen limitation is not the same object as viable reproductive return

The pollen-deficit result reinforces the process interpretation. An increase in investment slightly reduced the fractional viable pollen deficit in the focal far environment while decreasing absolute viable maternal offspring. The model therefore distinguishes pollen supply from reproductive return after accounting for allocation costs, mating route and inbreeding depression.

This is not a new general warning about pollen-limitation experiments. Reviews and syntheses have long emphasized that pollen limitation depends on ecological context, response variable and resource reallocation (Ashman et al., 2004; Knight et al., 2005, 2006). Empirical work has also shown that seed production can substantially overstate effective viable offspring when selfing and inbreeding depression are strong (Van Etten et al., 2015). The model result matters here because it links that measurement distinction to the same reproductive accounting that generates the evolutionary response.

For island comparisons, this implies that evidence for stronger pollen limitation should not automatically be interpreted as the magnitude of selection on floral attraction. The evolutionary value of an attraction trait depends on how additional pollen changes viable reproduction relative to its cost, not only on a fractional deficit.

## Strengths and limitations

The strongest part of the evidence is the separation of discovery and confirmation. The primary assurance-first result was rerun with new visitor histories and new demographic repeats under a frozen design, and the fixed-assurance intervention was independently repeated under the same new-history framework. The analysis preserves 64 visitor histories as the independent denominator rather than treating eight nested demographic repeats as additional ecological environments.

The confirmed claim is intentionally narrow. It applies to delayed selfing with assurance cost 0.5 and positive mutation in this synthetic model. The alternative prior-selfing setting shows that the sequence is not universal. The model does not estimate how common the mechanism is in nature, natural evolutionary rates, a calibrated geographic threshold, or a mediation fraction for realized selfing.

The fixed-assurance intervention blocks evolution of assurance capacity but does not remove selfing itself. Consequently, the correct conclusion is that *assurance evolution* is not necessary for investment decline under the tested conditions. It would be incorrect to conclude that reproductive assurance, selfing or mating system are irrelevant.

Several additional analyses remain supporting rather than primary. A continuous 13-level replenishment surface, broad local-selection parameter diagnostics, finite-versus-deterministic comparisons, mutation-history experiments and natural-island evidence bound mechanism and scope. The high-resolution positive-mutation deterministic/diffusion comparison remains unresolved and is excluded from the biological conclusion.

Natural validation also remains incomplete. Existing island studies rarely follow the same plant populations through measured changes in functional visitor exposure, pollen transfer, mating route, viable reproduction and inherited trait change. The strongest empirical test would therefore be longitudinal: measure those stages on the same populations before, during and after a change in pollinator connectivity or composition.

## Ecological implications

The results suggest a practical rule for interpreting multivariate island syndromes. Trait order should be treated as a description of realized dynamics, not as proof of mediation. If reproductive assurance changes before floral attraction, investigators should still ask whether the current pollinator environment has independently changed the return on attraction.

A second implication is that weak geographic divergence can underestimate evolutionary activity. In the broader original experiments, allowing assurance to evolve could reduce the near–far investment contrast because both environments changed. That interaction remains exploratory and was not part of the independent confirmation, but it illustrates why between-environment effect sizes should be distinguished from within-population evolutionary change.

More generally, plant ecological responses to pollinator loss are filtered through reproductive economics. Visitor supply determines opportunities for outcrossing, reproductive assurance changes how failed outcrossing can be compensated, allocation costs determine the price of attraction, and inbreeding depression determines the viable value of selfed offspring. Different traits can therefore respond to a common environmental change without forming a single causal sequence.

# Conclusions

Under sustained visitor-replenishment limitation, reproductive assurance can precede floral-investment decline without being necessary for that decline. The assurance-first sequence and the fixed-assurance investment response both passed prospectively frozen independent confirmation in the declared delayed-selfing, costly-assurance setting.

The upstream mechanism is ecological: visitor limitation directly reduces the reproductive return to pollinator-facing investment before plant traits evolve. The broader lesson is therefore not a universal order of selfing-syndrome evolution, but a distinction in inference. **Temporal precedence does not establish causal necessity.** Multivariate ecological evolution requires tests that separate order, upstream selective change and intervention-based dependence.
