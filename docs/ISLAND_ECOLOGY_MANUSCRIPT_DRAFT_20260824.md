# One perturbation, multiple island responses: state-dependent branching under pollinator functional simplification

## Scientifically validated primary manuscript — island ecology mainline — 2026-08-24

## Abstract

Islands repeatedly alter the abundance, identity and functional composition of pollinators, and broad comparative studies have identified recurrent island-associated shifts in plant reproductive traits. Yet established island plant lineages do not respond uniformly to apparently similar simplification or reorganization of pollinator function. Some show directional reproductive decline, others diverge among lineages or traits, and still others maintain reproduction despite altered interactions. We asked whether this diversity requires a different explanation for every island or can emerge from a common ecological architecture whose outcome depends on lineage state and local interaction context.

We used a frozen agent-based model in which plant lineages experienced a common change in pollinator functional opportunity while differing in pre-existing functional position and downstream interaction or reproductive context. We decomposed the model using factorial and residual ablations, independently replicated the strongest branch-generator boundary under a predeclared stochastic design, and then challenged the already-frozen response-state vocabulary against independent island plant–pollinator systems identified from a global literature screen. No external island outcome was used to tune parameters, choose random seeds, add mechanisms or redefine response states.

A common perturbation generated multiple downstream response states. Mixed-sign branching occurred in 0.4167 of matched runs in both the original and independently seeded blocks, but disappeared completely when pre-existing lineage functional-position heterogeneity was removed. Other tested residual single-factor removals retained branching. Local support strongly reallocated branch identity, changing 105 of 288 paired lineage response signs when removed. Network context was bidirectional: it rescued reproductive sign in 16 of 96 eligible declines and attenuated 85 of 96, but worsened 11 of 96. Autonomous assurance mainly attenuated propagation, reducing decline magnitude in 207 of 216 cases while producing no sign rescues in the independent block and none across a broadened 525-contrast envelope.

The global screen retained 54 geographic/system units, of which 13 entered a strict external challenge. These comprised three branching systems, six same-direction propagation systems, two buffering or alternative systems, one reproductive-axis-decoupling constraint and one retained falsification. All 11 generative challenges were covered or sign-compatible with response classes already generated by the frozen model. We therefore propose a **state-dependent view of island pollination responses**: island-associated biotic simplification does not imply a universal post-establishment trajectory. Instead, pre-existing lineage state determines the potential for response branching, local interaction context governs how perturbations propagate or are redirected, and reproductive filters can attenuate downstream consequences. Broad island syndromes may still emerge through colonization and persistence filters or aggregation across lineages, but they should not be interpreted as evidence that every established lineage follows the same ecological path.

**Keywords:** island biogeography; plant–pollinator interactions; functional traits; reproductive assurance; ecological networks; response heterogeneity; agent-based model; resilience

# 1. Introduction

Islands are natural experiments in ecological simplification, colonization and the reorganization of biotic interactions. Plant reproductive systems are especially sensitive to these changes because successful reproduction depends not only on abiotic conditions but also on the availability, identity and functional compatibility of pollinators. Comparative work has therefore sought recurrent island reproductive syndromes, including changes in self-compatibility, autonomous reproduction, floral form and pollination specialization. Such regularities are biologically important, but they combine at least two processes that need not generate the same predictions. Traits can influence **which lineages colonize, establish or persist on islands**, while already-established lineages can subsequently respond to altered pollinator function. The first is a filtering problem; the second is a post-establishment response problem.

The distinction matters because empirical island systems do not show one uniform downstream trajectory after pollinator communities change. Functional pollinator loss can propagate to pollen limitation, seed production and recruitment, as illustrated by bird-pollination natural experiments. In other cases, partner replacement or network reorganization maintains some reproductive function despite large changes in visitor identity. Still other systems show divergent responses among co-occurring or related plants exposed to a broadly shared perturbation. These contrasts are usually treated as system-specific outcomes. An alternative possibility is that they reflect a common ecological architecture in which the effect of a perturbation depends on the state of the lineage before the perturbation and on the local interaction context through which the perturbation propagates.

This possibility shifts the central island-ecology question. Rather than asking only whether island plants become more selfing, less specialized or morphologically simplified on average, we ask: **why does a common island-associated change in pollinator function produce different downstream responses among established plant lineages?** A simple conditional model suggests three layers. First, lineages occupy different positions in plant–pollinator functional space before the environment changes. Second, local interaction structure and partner support determine how a change in global pollinator opportunity is redistributed among lineages. Third, reproductive filters such as autonomous assurance can change how service loss propagates into reproduction. These layers predict that the same upstream perturbation can produce branching, same-direction propagation or buffering without requiring a separate model for every island (Fig. 1).

Agent-based models provide a useful experimental system for this question because candidate ecological mechanisms can be removed while holding the rest of the architecture fixed. That is stronger than simply fitting different parameter sets to different island examples. At the same time, flexible simulations can generate the same observable state through more than one route. We therefore froze the model architecture, stochastic envelopes, state definitions and falsification rules before the final external challenge. The purpose of this freezing is not to make model validation the primary biological question; it is to prevent external examples from being used post hoc to redefine mechanisms or rescue failed predictions.

We tested five linked hypotheses. **H1, the universal post-establishment response hypothesis**, predicts that a common island-like pollinator functional perturbation should push established lineages in one common downstream direction. It is rejected if opposite response signs coexist within the same matched environment. **H2, the state-dependent branching hypothesis**, predicts that pre-existing functional-position heterogeneity is required for within-environment response branching; removing that heterogeneity should eliminate branching while other single residual removals do not. **H3, the context-dependent propagation hypothesis**, predicts that local interaction context reallocates branch identity and can either rescue or worsen individual lineage responses. **H4, the autonomous-assurance buffering hypothesis**, predicts that reproductive assurance reduces the downstream effect of service decline; a stronger subprediction is that assurance can reverse the sign of decline. **H5, the cross-island recurrence hypothesis**, predicts that branching, same-direction propagation and buffering or alternative response states should recur across independent island systems without system-specific retuning of the model.

Together, these hypotheses distinguish an aggregate island syndrome from a universal within-lineage trajectory. The paper does not test whether island syndromes exist. It tests whether one ecological perturbation must produce one post-establishment response, and, if not, which conditional mechanisms generate the observed branching of outcomes.

# 2. Materials and Methods

## 2.1 Study design and frozen inference boundary

The study combines a controlled simulation experiment with qualitative external island-system challenges. The primary numerical results come from frozen model outputs and matched ablations. External island systems were not used to choose model parameters, random seeds, state definitions or mechanisms. New field data were not required for the primary simulation claim.

The model represents multiple plant lineages exposed to matched mainland-like and oceanic-like pollinator environments. Pollination opportunity emerges from interaction between plant and pollinator functional traits under a fixed visit-budget formulation. Plant lineages can differ in initial standardized matching-trait position and, depending on experimental layer, trait adjustment, local support, pollinator dependency, assurance ceiling, assurance responsiveness and partner effectiveness.

The standardized plant matching trait is an abstract relative functional coordinate. It is not assigned post hoc to corolla length, colour, nectar guides or any single empirical trait. This boundary is important because the model can identify the ecological role of relative starting position without establishing which measured trait carries that position in every real island system.

## 2.2 H1: testing whether one perturbation forces one response direction

We defined within-run mixed-sign branching as the occurrence of both positive and negative lineage reproductive responses to the same matched mainland-like to island-like environmental contrast. A universal post-establishment response would predict that lineages within a matched run respond in the same direction. The frozen state atlas records branching, same-direction and buffering states generated by the model without system-specific retuning.

## 2.3 H2: residual ablation of the branch generator

A downstream factorial first tested four mechanism families: local support, dependency heterogeneity, assurance responsiveness and partner effectiveness. Branching persisted even when all four were fixed OFF, indicating that the generator of opposite lineage responses lay upstream of those modifiers.

The residual experiment therefore fixed the four downstream modifiers OFF and manipulated three remaining lineage-level sources: initial trait-position heterogeneity, trait-adjustment heterogeneity and assurance-ceiling heterogeneity. A residual factor was considered necessary within the declared model if its removal eliminated mixed-sign runs and reduced mean within-run branching balance to zero while other single-factor removals retained branching.

The strongest boundary was tested once in an independent frozen block using seed `90260825`, four replicates per saturation, 24 lineages, 120 steps and saturation values 1, 2 and 3. The design, decision rule and stop rule were specified before execution. No external target or empirical input selected the seed. The first successfully executed scientific result was retained and further seed searching was closed.

## 2.4 H3: local interaction context and branch allocation

We evaluated local network context by comparing local support OFF versus ON under matched opportunity networks while autonomous assurance was disabled. Analyses of strong rescue were restricted to lineages for which global opportunity declined and support-OFF reproduction was negative. We distinguished three outcomes: magnitude rescue, in which the decline became less negative; sign rescue, in which the response crossed to zero or positive; and worsening, in which the response became more negative.

Because rescue and worsening were both allowed outcomes, the experiment tested context-dependent branch allocation and buffering capacity rather than assuming that additional support must be protective.

## 2.5 H4: autonomous assurance

Autonomous assurance was tested in matched simulations with the assurance route enabled or disabled while upstream effective-service changes were identical. We distinguished magnitude attenuation from strong sign rescue. Stability was evaluated in an independent frozen block and in a broadened local-support envelope. A sign rescue observed in an earlier stochastic block was retained historically but not promoted unless it replicated.

## 2.6 H5: external island-system challenge

A global literature screen retained 54 geographic/system units as the screening denominator. Thirteen systems met a strict state-challenge contract based on source-locked evidence. The strict set contained three branching systems, six same-direction propagation systems, two buffering or alternative systems, one reproductive-axis-decoupling constraint and one retained falsification.

The external comparison is intentionally qualitative at the state level. The 13 systems are not a random prevalence sample, and their counts are not interpreted as global frequencies of response states. Nor are they 13 independent validations of one model mechanism. They test whether the already-frozen response vocabulary is broad enough to encompass recurrent ecological outcomes without retuning.

The strict branching systems were the Izu multi-taxon Hiraiwa–Ushimaru system, Caribbean Gesneriaceae and the Canary Islands Teide honeybee-network experiment. Same-direction systems were Ogasawara *Psychotria homalosperma*, New Zealand *Rhabdothamnus solandri*, the Guam–Saipan bird-loss natural experiment, Seychelles invasive-ant disruption, Mauritius *Roussea simplex* invasive-ant disruption and Bahamas *Pavonia bahamensis* after hurricane-associated pollinator decline. Buffering or alternative systems were Hawaiian lobelioids after bird extinction and California Channel Islands *Nicotiana glauca*. Puerto Rico–Mona *Guaiacum sanctum* was retained as a reproductive-axis-decoupling constraint. Dominica *Heliconia* was retained as a failed signed-position projection.

## 2.7 Supporting inference diagnostics

After the ecological mechanism tests were frozen, state-separability diagnostics were used as an interpretation guard. They quantify whether a given response state is unique to a tested mechanism contrast. These diagnostics are supporting rather than primary results. Their purpose is to prevent, for example, same-direction response from being interpreted as proof of homogeneous starting states or magnitude attenuation from being interpreted as unique evidence for autonomous assurance.

# 3. Results

## 3.1 H1: one island-like perturbation did not force one plant response

The frozen model generated more than one downstream response class under one architecture. In the original residual block, mixed-sign branching occurred in 5 of 12 matched runs, giving a mixed-sign run fraction of 0.4167. The remaining 7 of 12 runs were same-direction despite heterogeneous starting states. Thus a common change in pollinator functional opportunity can produce both lineage branching and same-direction outcomes without changing the model from island to island.

The independent robustness block reproduced the same full-model mixed-sign frequency of 0.4167. H1 was therefore rejected: a common island-like perturbation does not imply a universal post-establishment response direction (Table 1).

## 3.2 H2: pre-existing functional position was the replicated minimal branch generator

The downstream factorial showed that two-sided branching persisted when local support, dependency heterogeneity, assurance responsiveness and partner effectiveness were all fixed OFF. These downstream mechanisms were therefore not required to generate opposite response signs.

In the residual ablation, the full model had mixed-sign frequency 0.4167 and mean within-run branching balance 0.2569. Removing initial trait-position heterogeneity reduced both to zero and changed 37 paired lineage signs. Removing trait-adjustment heterogeneity retained mixed-sign frequency 0.4167 and the same branching balance, with only two paired sign changes. Removing assurance-ceiling heterogeneity also retained mixed-sign frequency 0.4167 and produced no paired sign changes.

The independent block reproduced the same causal boundary. Full-model mixed-sign frequency was 0.4167 with mean within-run balance 0.2917. Removing initial functional-position heterogeneity again reduced both quantities to zero and changed 44 paired lineage signs. Removing trait-adjustment heterogeneity or assurance-ceiling heterogeneity retained mixed-sign frequency 0.4167.

Across two independently seeded frozen blocks, pre-existing lineage functional position was therefore the only tested residual factor whose removal eliminated within-run response-sign branching. Within the declared model, H2 was supported and independently reproduced (Fig. 2).

## 3.3 H3 and H4: local context reallocated branches, whereas assurance mainly attenuated magnitude

Although local support was not the origin of branching, it strongly changed branch identity. Removing local support changed 105 of 288 paired lineage response signs, compared with 13 of 288 for partner effectiveness, one for assurance responsiveness and none for dependency heterogeneity.

The independent network-context experiment demonstrated why local support should not be described as a universal buffer. Among 96 eligible negative support-OFF reproductive contrasts, support ON attenuated 85, crossed the sign boundary in 16 and worsened 11. H3 was therefore supported in a bidirectional form: local interaction context reallocates response branches and has genuine buffering capacity, but it can also amplify decline.

Autonomous assurance occupied a different response regime. In the independent block, 216 lineages experienced upstream service decline. Assurance reduced the magnitude of reproductive decline in 207 of 216 cases but produced no sign rescues. At saturation values 1, 2 and 3, magnitude attenuation occurred in 71/75, 73/76 and 63/65 declines, respectively, while sign rescue remained zero. A broadened support envelope similarly produced zero sign rescues among 525 eligible declines.

For H4, magnitude attenuation was supported but robust sign rescue was rejected. Assurance robustly attenuated reproductive decline magnitude, but the stronger hypothesis that it reliably reverses the qualitative response sign was not supported (Fig. 3).

## 3.4 H5: response-state diversity recurred across independent island systems

The 54-unit global screen yielded 13 strict external systems. Eleven were generative challenges: three branching, six same-direction propagation and two buffering or alternative cases. All 11 were covered or sign-compatible with response classes already present in the frozen model.

The three branching systems provided independent examples in which one broad ecological context was associated with divergent downstream plant responses: the Izu multi-taxon system, Caribbean Gesneriaceae and the Canary Islands Teide honeybee-network experiment. The six propagation systems showed cases in which disruption moved through pollination and reproduction in a common direction, including bird functional loss in New Zealand and the Marianas, invasive-ant disruption in Seychelles and Mauritius, an access asymmetry in Ogasawara and hurricane-associated pollination loss in the Bahamas. Hawaiian lobelioids and Channel Islands *Nicotiana* provided buffering or alternative-response boundaries.

Two systems were intentionally not counted as generative successes. Puerto Rico–Mona *Guaiacum* retained a reproductive-axis-decoupling state because a large difference in visitor context coexisted with similar self/outcross seed-set indices while other reproductive axes differed. Dominica *Heliconia* retained a failed signed-position projection: the predeclared negative direction was not recovered and the mapping was not retuned.

H5 was therefore supported at the response-state level. The external series shows that branching, propagation and buffering are recurrent island ecological outcomes, but it does not establish that one empirical mechanism generates all of them (Fig. 4; Table 2).

## 3.5 Supporting inference boundary

The state-separability analysis reinforced the claim boundary rather than supplying the main biological result. Mixed-sign branching was highly specific but insensitive for initial functional-position heterogeneity within the tested intervention family: specificity was 1.0 but sensitivity 0.4167. Same-direction response was therefore weak evidence for uniform starting states because 0.5833 of heterogeneity-ON runs were also non-mixed. Network-context sign rescue was similarly specific but rare, whereas magnitude attenuation occurred commonly under both network context and assurance.

These diagnostics show why external state compatibility cannot be promoted to empirical mechanism identification. They are retained as supporting evidence and Supplementary detail, while the protected failed and narrowed claims remain explicit (Table 3).

# 4. Discussion

## 4.1 Aggregate island syndromes can coexist with lineage-level branching

The central ecological result is that one island-like functional perturbation does not require one post-establishment plant trajectory. The frozen model generated opposite lineage responses under the same matched environmental shift, and the source of that branching was not downstream dependency or reproductive assurance. Instead, pre-existing functional position determined whether lineages could occupy different response branches.

This does not contradict the existence of island reproductive syndromes. Macroecological patterns can arise because some reproductive strategies increase colonization probability, because some lineages persist more successfully after arrival, or because repeated ecological responses accumulate across lineages. Such processes change the composition of island floras. Our question is conditional on presence: once lineages are established, how does a changed pollinator-functional environment propagate through plants that begin in different ecological states?

The distinction resolves an apparent tension. An island flora can show an aggregate enrichment of self-compatible or generalized reproductive strategies while individual established lineages still show decline, compensation, trait-specific divergence or little response. The aggregate pattern and lineage-level branching operate at different inferential levels. The present results therefore reject a universal **post-establishment trajectory**, not the broader possibility of recurrent island syndromes.

## 4.2 Functional starting state determines branch potential

The strongest mechanistic result is the replicated loss of branching when initial functional-position heterogeneity is removed. This suggests that response direction is fundamentally relational. The ecological effect of a changing pollinator environment depends not only on how much the environment changes but also on where the plant lineage already lies relative to the relevant functional opportunity landscape.

This interpretation is more general than any single floral trait. Corolla length, nectar guides, colour, phenology or multivariate floral architecture may each contribute to matching in particular systems, but the current model does not identify one universal empirical axis. Treating the standardized coordinate as a named trait after observing a favourable empirical outcome would overstate the evidence. The unresolved empirical identity of the functional starting position is therefore a deliberate boundary, not a missing parameter to be filled with a proxy.

Two independent morphology analyses completed before the v12 branch-generator result are directionally consistent with starting-state dependence: island response shape is compressed relative to mainland starting size in both data sets under OLS analysis. However, measurement-error and errors-in-variables limits prevent those comparisons from being promoted to a pooled coefficient or direct validation of the pollinator-matching mechanism. They are best viewed as supporting context for a future outcome-blind test of signed plant position relative to a source-native pollinator functional center.

## 4.3 Local interaction context governs propagation, whereas assurance mainly dampens it

The model separates branch generation from branch allocation. Local support can change the sign of individual lineage responses even though branching remains possible without it. This means that network context does not create the underlying possibility of opposite responses; it changes which lineages realize which branch.

The presence of both rescue and worsening is ecologically important. Network reorganization is often discussed under the language of resilience, rewiring or redundancy. Those concepts can imply protection if read too loosely. Here, added local support rescued 16 of 96 eligible negative responses but worsened 11. The same class of contextual mechanism can therefore compensate one lineage and disadvantage another. A more precise description is a **bidirectional branch allocator with buffering capacity**.

Autonomous assurance operates downstream in a different way. It repeatedly reduced the magnitude of decline but did not robustly convert negative responses into maintained or positive reproduction. This distinction helps separate two forms of apparent resilience. A system can appear buffered because reproductive decline is smaller, or because the qualitative response sign is rescued. The model shows that these states need not arise from the same route.

The resulting ecological ordering is hierarchical: pre-existing state generates branch potential, local interaction structure redistributes propagation, and reproductive assurance dampens the magnitude of downstream consequences. This hierarchy is more informative than treating all heterogeneity as interchangeable modifiers of a single island effect.

## 4.4 Cross-island recurrence supports a response architecture, not a universal mechanism

The external island series extends the ecological relevance of the model beyond one focal archipelago. The strict systems include pollinator loss, partner replacement, managed-honeybee effects, invasive-ant interference and hurricane-associated pollination disruption. Despite this mechanistic diversity, the observed outcomes repeatedly fall into branching, same-direction propagation and buffering or alternative response states already available in the frozen model.

The comparison should not be interpreted as a prevalence estimate. The 13 systems were admitted through a strict evidence gate, not sampled randomly from the world's islands. Nor should their state compatibility be read as proof that the synthetic initial-position mechanism operates in every system. A same-direction natural experiment can be accommodated by the model even when the real cause is bird extinction, invasive-ant interference or another system-specific process.

The value of the external challenge is therefore architectural. It shows that the model is not explaining a response class peculiar to one dataset. Independent systems repeatedly occupy the broad state classes expected from a conditional post-establishment response framework. At the same time, Guaiacum and Dominica show why a useful architecture needs protected exceptions. Guaiacum prevents multiple reproductive axes from being collapsed into one buffered/unbuffered label, and Dominica demonstrates that an empirical signed-position projection can fail without forcing the synthetic branch-generator result to be retuned.

## 4.5 What remains unresolved empirically

Three empirical questions now follow directly from the paper.

First, the synthetic functional starting position requires a direct real-world analogue. The highest-value test is an outcome-blind signed-position design in which a plant matching trait and a source-native pollinator functional center are defined on compatible units before downstream reproductive response is inspected. The preferred Izu route remains blocked by missing source-native pollinator functional trait values and quantitative plant-specific partner weighting. That absence should be preserved rather than filled with guild midpoints, visitor identity or body-size proxies.

Second, the network-context mechanism needs a matched empirical test that links local partner context to effective pollination rather than visitation alone. Retrospective audits identify partial bridge systems, but strict rate-weighted effective-service mapping remains incomplete. A named real-island claim that network context caused buffering therefore remains future work.

Third, no current external system closes the full empirical chain from pollinator functional change through effective service and reproductive dependency or assurance to downstream floral or reproductive response on compatible matched units. Several systems provide adjacent links, but a complete bridge remains absent. This is a useful design result: the next study should target the missing linkage rather than simply add more morphology-only or visitation-only island examples.

None of these unresolved questions blocks the present submission because the current paper's primary claim is conditional and synthetic: it identifies how multiple island response trajectories can arise under a frozen ecological architecture and shows that those trajectories recur across independent island systems.

## 4.6 Inference boundary

Observable response states are not equally informative about their generating mechanism. Mixed-sign branching is informative when present but need not appear in every heterogeneous run. Same-direction response therefore cannot be used to infer homogeneous lineages. Likewise, decline attenuation alone cannot distinguish network-context compensation from autonomous assurance because both can reduce magnitude.

This is the appropriate role of the state-separability analysis in the current paper: it prevents the ecological synthesis from becoming stronger than the evidence. The external island challenge establishes recurrence of response states; it does not identify the same real-world causal mechanism in every system.

---

# 5. Conclusion

Island-associated pollinator simplification is better viewed as a common perturbation acting on different ecological starting states than as a force imposing one universal post-establishment plant trajectory. In the frozen ABM, pre-existing lineage functional position is the replicated minimal generator of within-environment response branching. Local interaction context then reallocates branch identity and can either rescue or worsen propagation, whereas autonomous assurance mainly attenuates the magnitude of reproductive decline.

Independent island systems repeatedly occupy the same broad response classes—branching, same-direction propagation and buffering or alternative states—without system-specific retuning of the model. Those recurrences support a state-dependent island-response architecture while the retained Guaiacum constraint and Dominica failure prevent a universal-mechanism interpretation.

The resulting island-ecology synthesis is therefore conditional rather than syndrome-only: **aggregate island syndromes can coexist with lineage-level branching because colonization and persistence determine which states arrive, while functional starting state and local ecological context determine how established lineages respond after pollinator environments change.**
