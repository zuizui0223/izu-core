# Deep literature synthesis: what does the plant “island syndrome” actually mean?

Updated: 2026-08-24

## Purpose

This review is a manuscript-facing synthesis for the island-ecology mainline. It asks a narrower question than “do island syndromes exist?”:

> **Which components of the plant island syndrome are best interpreted as colonization/assembly filters, which represent in-situ evolution after colonization, and which are ecological responses to reorganized interaction environments?**

That distinction is essential for the current manuscript because the frozen ABM addresses the third layer: **post-establishment response to a changed pollinator-functional environment**.

---

# 1. “Island syndrome” is an umbrella term, not one causal process

Recent syntheses define island syndromes as recurrent suites of differences between island and mainland organisms, but plant syndromes span several biological levels. Whittaker, Fernández-Palacios & Matthews (2023) review reproductive, morphological and interaction-level components including self-compatibility, floral shifts, pollination-mechanism changes and pollination-network generalism. Ciarle & Burns (2024/2025) similarly review the plant island syndrome in New Zealand's outlying islands and emphasize that different candidate syndrome components have very different empirical support.

For the present manuscript, the literature is most coherent if divided into three inferential layers:

1. **Colonization / assembly filtering** — traits alter arrival, establishment or persistence probability, changing which lineages are represented in island floras.
2. **In-situ evolutionary change** — a lineage changes after colonization relative to its mainland ancestor/source.
3. **Post-establishment ecological response / interaction reorganization** — an established lineage responds to current changes in pollinator functional composition, network context or reproductive buffering.

An aggregate island flora can therefore show a strong syndrome even if established lineages do not share one response trajectory.

---

# 2. Strongest assembly-level evidence: reproductive assurance and Baker's law

## 2.1 The narrow version of Baker's law is well supported

Baker's law is most defensible when interpreted as an advantage of **capacity for uniparental reproduction during colonization**, not as a prediction that island populations must exhibit a uniformly high realized selfing rate.

Pannell et al. (2015; DOI `10.1111/nph.13539`) explicitly narrow the law to enrichment of self-fertility/uniparental reproductive capacity in colonizing situations. Barrett (2015; DOI `10.1111/j.1461-0248.2014.01743.x`, conceptual review of colonizing mating systems) likewise stresses that dispersal, establishment and later demographic phases can impose different sieves.

Grossenbacher et al. (2017; DOI `10.1111/nph.14534`) provide a strong global comparative test: across >1500 species from Asteraceae, Brassicaceae and Solanaceae, 66% of island species were self-compatible versus 41% of mainland species. Their interpretation is explicitly a **colonization filter**.

Zell et al. (2025; DOI `10.1111/nph.20234`) broaden this with 3222 flowering plant species from 169 families. Self-compatibility was strongly associated with island occurrence; longer lifespan and generalized pollination syndrome also increased island occurrence in interaction with breeding system, while arrival opportunity and conserved taxonomic effects remained important.

### Manuscript implication

These studies make it unsafe to frame the current paper as rejecting island syndromes. They instead support a sharper statement:

> **Strong aggregate reproductive syndromes can arise because colonization and establishment are selective before any post-establishment response is observed.**

This is the key literature support for separating the current ABM question from Baker's-law assembly effects.

---

# 3. The dioecy/self-compatibility “paradox” shows why one trajectory is implausible

Island floras can simultaneously show enrichment for self-compatible colonists and elevated frequencies of gender dimorphism in some regions. This is not necessarily contradictory if the two patterns occur at different stages.

Pannell et al. (2015) note that Baker's law concerns colonization capacity, whereas subsequent selection can favour outcrossing-promoting systems. Grossenbacher et al. (2017) also note that post-colonization evolution of dioecy or other outcrossing mechanisms can obscure a colonization-filter signature.

Lord (2015; DOI `10.1093/aobpla/plv095`) reviewed 321 species across Southern Ocean islands. Self-compatibility was very high among species with data, but anemophily and gender dimorphism were not clearly elevated relative to appropriate reference floras; source flora and climate were important confounders. This is a useful caution against treating every recurrent island trait as one universal evolutionary response.

### Manuscript implication

The reproductive island syndrome itself already contains evidence for **stage dependence and pathway dependence**. This strongly supports the manuscript's distinction between:

- which reproductive states reach/persist on islands;
- how established lineages respond after the pollinator environment changes.

# 4. Floral morphology is not a universal directional island syndrome

## 4.1 Pacific sister-taxon test: no universal flower-size reduction

Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson (2020; DOI `10.1086/709018`) analysed 556 plant species in 136 phylogenetically independent island-mainland sister contrasts. Contrary to the common expectation that insect-pollinated island flowers become smaller, there was **no overall directional reduction in flower size**. Smaller island flowers occurred in some archipelagos (e.g. Galápagos and Revillagigedo), while family-level responses differed in direction or were absent.

Their key inference is especially important here: if island floras are dominated by smaller flowers, **ecological filtering may explain that aggregate pattern better than universal post-colonization evolutionary dwarfing**.

## 4.2 Southwest Pacific: in-situ evolution exists, but it is conditional on starting size and pollination mode

Ciarle, Burns & Mologni (2025; DOI `10.1093/aob/mcaf005`) analysed 129 independent colonization events across ten Southwest Pacific archipelagos. Animal-pollinated flowers followed an island-rule pattern, whereas wind-pollinated flowers showed gigantism rather than the same trajectory. The result persisted after controlling for breeding system, mainland source pool, taxonomic differentiation, family and island type.

This is not evidence for one directional island floral syndrome. It is evidence that **starting state and functional context can determine the direction of island evolution**.

Méndez (2025; DOI `10.1093/aob/mcaf053`) explicitly frames the central inferential distinction as environmental filtering versus in-situ evolutionary change.

### Manuscript implication

This literature is unusually close to the current synthetic result. It supports replacing a naive statement such as

> island flowers become smaller

with

> **island floral evolution can be conditional on mainland starting state, pollination mode, clade and archipelago.**

The current ABM extends that conditional logic from a morphology pattern to a mechanistic post-establishment response architecture.

# 5. Pollination-network “island syndrome”: simpler interaction environments, but not one topological outcome

Traveset et al. (2016; DOI `10.1111/geb.12362`) compared 52 quantitative plant-pollinator networks worldwide (23 continental-island, 18 oceanic-island, 11 mainland). Oceanic island networks were generally smaller and topologically simplified, with lower interaction diversity and higher plant niche overlap. Isolation and elevation affected network properties. However, many structural properties were otherwise similar across origins, so “island network simplification” is not equivalent to every network metric declining.

A Canary-versus-Balearic comparison likewise found fewer species on the oceanic island but not fewer interactions; super-generalists increased connectance and nestedness. Thus fewer species can coexist with compensatory rewiring or greater generalized connectivity.

Wang et al. (2020; DOI `10.1038/s41598-020-70954-7`) found a highly generalized pollination system on Yongxing Island: plants were visited by more pollinator species than classical pollination syndromes predicted, and five floral traits explained only 22.5% of visitor preferences. Restrictive flowers were more specialized, but most floral characters did not map cleanly onto ecological specialization.

Abe (2006; DOI `10.1093/aob/mcl081`) documented an Ogasawara pollination syndrome including relatively high dioecy, subdued floral colours and dominance of small solitary pollinators on less-invaded satellite islands, while also identifying specialist pollination niches and major disruption by introduced honeybees.

### Manuscript implication

Island interaction networks often become **depauperate, functionally reorganized and more overlapping/generalized**, but rewiring can buffer some routes while degrading others. That empirical ambiguity maps naturally onto the model result that local context is a **bidirectional branch allocator**, not a universal buffer.

---

# 6. Functional diversity, not species richness alone, is the mechanistic bridge needed here

The most direct empirical bridge to the present model is Hiraiwa & Ushimaru (2024; DOI `10.1111/1365-2435.14527`). Across 40 spatiotemporally variable coastal networks spanning Japanese continental and oceanic islands, low pollinator functional diversity—especially low abundance of long-tongued pollinators—reduced community-level functional specialization and flower-pollinator trait matching, and lower matching reduced community-level pollination success. Pollinator species diversity itself did not explain trait matching or pollination success in the same way.

This matters because it justifies the paper's upstream variable as **pollinator functional simplification/reorganization**, rather than the weaker and often misleading label “pollinator species loss”.

### Manuscript implication

The island-syndrome literature should therefore be connected to the ABM through:

`island isolation / disharmony`
→ `pollinator functional composition changes`
→ `trait matching / interaction opportunity changes`
→ `lineage-specific response`

rather than:

`island`
→ `fewer pollinator species`
→ `one universal floral response`.

---

# 7. A functional island-biogeography synthesis already points toward conditionality

Schrader et al. (2021; DOI `10.1111/brv.12782`) argue for a functional island-biogeography programme rather than treating island occurrence only taxonomically. Their review identifies self-compatibility, clonal reproduction, generalized/easily accessible flowers and other reproductive or interaction traits as filters associated with island occurrence.

Whittaker et al. (2023; DOI `10.1093/oso/9780198868569.003.0011`) conclude that plant island syndromes exist across several trait domains but emphasize that systematic comparative support is uneven across candidate syndromes.

Ciarle & Burns (2024/2025; DOI `10.1080/0028825X.2024.2377418`) similarly review the island syndrome in New Zealand's outlying islands and show that some classic predictions have evidence while others remain poorly tested or geographically contingent.

### Manuscript implication

The literature is moving away from asking only “what is the island phenotype?” and toward asking **which processes and functional constraints generate each component**. The current manuscript can sit directly in that transition.

# 8. Revised conceptual model for the manuscript

The term **plant island syndrome** should be decomposed as follows:

```text
MAINLAND SOURCE POOL
    |
    |  dispersal + arrival opportunity
    v
COLONIZATION / ASSEMBLY FILTER
    |  self-compatibility
    |  lifespan / clonality
    |  generalized reproductive accessibility
    v
ESTABLISHED ISLAND FLORA
    |
    |  altered pollinator functional composition
    |  interaction-network reorganization
    v
POST-ESTABLISHMENT RESPONSE
    |
    +--> lineage starting functional position -> branch potential
    |
    +--> local interaction context -> branch allocation / rescue / worsening
    |
    +--> reproductive assurance -> magnitude attenuation
    v
OBSERVED LINEAGE TRAJECTORIES

Repeated aggregation across lineages and time
    -> macroecological “island syndrome”
```

This resolves the apparent contradiction between strong global island syndromes and heterogeneous lineage responses.

# 10. Stronger novelty statement after literature review

A defensible novelty statement is:

> **Plant island syndromes are increasingly understood as composites of assembly filters, in-situ evolutionary changes and reorganized biotic interactions. Existing comparative studies establish that these components can be strong but conditional: self-compatibility is enriched through colonization filtering, flower-size evolution varies with starting size and pollination mode, and oceanic pollination networks simplify and reorganize without converging on one topological outcome. What remains less developed is a mechanistic account of why established lineages exposed to a comparable pollinator-functional perturbation occupy different response trajectories. Our frozen ABM addresses that missing post-establishment layer by separating branch generation by pre-existing functional state from branch allocation by local interaction context and downstream attenuation by reproductive assurance.**

This is stronger and safer than claiming to disprove the island syndrome.

## Boundary

This synthesis does not convert literature-level recurrence into evidence that all islands share one causal mechanism. It is used to sharpen the distinction among assembly filtering, in-situ evolution and post-establishment response, and therefore to define the current paper's ecological contribution more precisely.
