# Prior art: assurance first, floral returns and realized response

**11 November 2026.** Primary-literature readback for Chapter 2; no new model run or analysis. The question is not whether selfing can rescue fertilization. It is whether that benefit predicts the order of evolutionary responses, whether assurance evolution is necessary for floral decline, and whether favourable returns result in inherited change.

## What the literature already distinguishes

**1. Reproductive insurance, flowering-time schedules and evolutionary order.** [Lloyd (1992), selection model](https://doi.org/10.1086/297041) derived different conditions for prior, competing and delayed selfing as functions of foregone outcross seeds and pollen export. These labels describe the timing of selfing relative to outcross opportunities *within reproduction*; they do not imply that a heritable increase in assurance occurred before a heritable change in investment. [Kalisz, Vogler & Hanley (2004), Collinsia](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02776) documented context-dependent reproductive assurance and mixed mating during pollinator failure. Conversely, in ten Aquilegia populations over two years, the seed-production gain from selfing was outweighed by seed discounting and inbreeding depression ([Herlihy & Eckert 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11907577/)). Thus seed rescue is a plausible immediate benefit, not proof that early acquisition improves later survival. Comparative work on Collinsia also distinguished autonomous-selfing timing, floral dimensions and realized mating ([Kalisz et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21980191/)); associations among species are not an intervention assigning evolutionary order.

**2. Maternal and paternal returns need not track one another.** In Tuberaria guttata, emasculation, supplementary cross-pollination and maternal seed production showed that assurance can weaken pollinator-mediated selection for larger flowers ([Teixido & Aizen 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcz014)); this did not measure outcross siring. In Mimulus ringens, display manipulation and paternity assignment showed different responses through the two sexual functions: maternal success per flower was stable, whereas outcross siring per flower declined as more flowers were displayed simultaneously. Whole-plant fitness increased with diminishing returns. Pollinators visiting more flowers on the same plant can discount export, illustrating why a positive paternal return cannot be assumed at every display size ([Karron & Mitchell 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278290/)). Maternal assurance does not rule out a paternal benefit, but its sign, magnitude and trait dependence are empirical questions. A field test should vary attraction and measure maternal viable seeds and **outcrossed** offspring sired elsewhere for the same individual. Distinguish per-flower from whole-plant returns; measure within-plant movements and pollen limitation. The model's investment is allocation, not a count of simultaneous flowers.

**3. Shared shortage does not specify the same selection gradient.** In M. guttatus, positive selection on flower length under open pollination persisted with pollen supplementation, whereas exclusion favoured relatively narrow corollas and smaller anther–stigma distances ([pollen addition/exclusion experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18005321/)). In L. siphilitica, experimentally reducing pollination produced maternal selection *for taller inflorescences and more vibrant petals* where ambient controls showed no significant pollinator-mediated selection ([field experiment, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37953983/)). Neither result is an observed multigeneration response to selection. Conversely, in an experimental evolution study, different visitor treatments in Brassica rapa produced divergence in floral signals and self-pollination ([Gervasi & Schiestl 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms14691)). This supports separating visitor identity and attraction quantity; it does not validate the model's matching optimum or its numerical predictive skills in nature.

**4. Changes in flower size, mating ability and standing variation.** In M. guttatus, pollinator removal produced pronounced changes in autonomous seed set and anther–stigma separation ([Bodbyl Roels & Kelly 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21884055/)); the follow-up genomic experiment found reduced diversity ([Busch et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9543508/)). These overlapping experiments should not be counted as independent evidence. In another 20-population mating-treatment experiment, size decline under selfing largely reflected inbreeding depression, despite adaptive changes in autogamy ([Tusuubira & Kelly 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2024.1378568)). Capsella provides a contrast: standing regulatory variation at a growth locus contributed to smaller petals ([Sicard et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5137693/)). A small flower is therefore neither an assay of selfing nor a unique readout of allocation; polygenic architecture, expression and inbreeding can also matter. The present model does not recreate all of these mechanisms.

**5. Selection is not realized evolution; this principle predates the model.** In Lobelia, combining genetic variance–covariance estimates with field selection gradients predicted weaker or opposite responses for several floral traits ([quantitative-genetic study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15154549/)). These were predictions, not mother–offspring observations of a favoured allele disappearing. The distinction to emphasize here is the source-linked contrast between an expected frequency response and actual transmission, and between segregating variation and support for a *specified directional target*. This is more specific than stating that variance constrains evolution.

**6. Island comparisons provide the puzzle rather than its causal solution.** In three sampled families self-compatible species were overrepresented on islands ([Grossenbacher et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.14534)); a broader comparative study linked island occurrence to self-compatibility alongside arrival and other traits ([Zell et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39517112/)). Self-compatibility is not autonomous selfing rate or evidence that assurance repeatedly arose *after arrival*. Across 136 independent Pacific island–mainland contrasts there was no uniform decline in flower size ([Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32552102/)); Southwest Pacific comparisons separated pollination-mode-dependent size patterns from display ([Ciarle et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39825751/)). These comparisons are compatible with heterogeneous floral outcomes and colonization filtering; they cannot identify parental allocation mechanisms or demonstrate within-population diversity maintenance.

## Novelty and falsifiability

What the repository adds is a set of distinct questions answered under declared interventions, not a new universal pollinator-decline law:

- **Order versus benefit:** compare observed threshold precedence with the [assigned transient-expression-order test](CHAPTER2_ORDER_EXPRESSION_FULL_COHORT_RESULTS_20261009.md). Neither the maternal return nor an observed A-first trajectory estimates the effect of acquiring A first. The assigned-order interaction was small relative to its prespecified equivalence margin; that estimand is an environmental difference in schedule effects, not an equivalence test on every possible order benefit.
- **Necessity versus modification:** investment can decline when expressed A is fixed (selfing still possible). Allowing A to evolve changes the difference between visitor regimes; this does not mean it strengthens decline in every environment. See the [two-proposition audit](CHAPTER2_TWO_PROPOSITIONS_PROOF_AND_RESULT_20261016.md) and [fresh-founder registered comparison](CHAPTER2_Q1_FRESH_FOUNDER_RESULTS_20261016.md).
- **Repeatability versus access:** [matched endpoints](CHAPTER2_MATCHED_AI_REPEATABILITY_20261019.md), [known-source prediction](CHAPTER2_FOUNDER_PREDICTABILITY_RESULTS_20261021.md), and the [loss-conditioned inheritance readback](CHAPTER2_TWO_PROPOSITIONS_PROOF_AND_RESULT_20261016.md) answer different questions. Agreement on an A endpoint need not imply selection of a uniform strength; loss of a low-I allele despite favourable expected transmission cannot be turned into a population-wide drift fraction. Whether flower-size divergence in nature reflects altered allocation remains testable, as does the contribution of outcross siring.
