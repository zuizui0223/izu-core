# Reproductive assurance compresses floral-investment divergence under pollinator limitation

**Article type:** Letter  
**Target journal:** Ecology Letters  
**Running title:** Assurance compresses floral divergence  
**Status:** submission-focused manuscript; long-form process manuscript retained separately as provenance  
**Figures:** 4  
**Tables:** 0

## Abstract

Pollinator limitation is often associated with increased reproductive assurance and reduced floral attraction, encouraging a serial interpretation in which breeding-system evolution causes floral simplification. We tested whether reproductive assurance is actually required for reduced floral investment and, if not, how its evolution changes the phenotypic contrast between pollination environments. We used an explicit plant–pollinator model with continuing visitor establishment and loss, pollen transfer, maternal and paternal reproduction, selfing, inbreeding depression, inheritance and finite demography. In a prospectively frozen experiment using 64 independent new visitor histories and eight nested demographic repeats, floral investment still declined under low visitor replenishment when assurance capacity was fixed across four reproductive settings. Fixed far-minus-near investment contrasts ranged from −0.302 to −0.441. Allowing assurance to evolve consistently reduced those contrasts (evolving-minus-fixed interactions +0.101 to +0.220; all 95% visitor-history bootstrap intervals above zero). The attenuation arose mainly because assurance evolution pulled higher-replenishment populations toward lower investment: 78–90% of the interaction was localized to additional near-side decline, with smaller far-side relief. The higher-replenishment environment was still pollen-limited: an independent fixed-plant snapshot-400 assay found about 22% of ovules fertilized by outcross pollen, with about 39% still unfilled after autonomous selfing. These are fixed-state measurements, not terminal values from evolving populations. A corrected rare-mutant analysis showed the corresponding local mechanism: increasing assurance weakened investment selection far more strongly under higher than lower replenishment because maternal outcross and paternal pollen-export returns remained available to lose. Reproductive assurance is therefore not required for pollinator-limitation-driven investment decline, but its evolution can compress the phenotypic signature of environmental divergence. In these incompletely pollinated model regimes, breeding-system evolution can mask part of the contrast in evolved floral investment.

# Introduction

Pollinator decline can alter both mating opportunities and selection on floral traits. A common interpretation of the selfing syndrome is sequential: reduced pollination favours reproductive assurance, and increased selfing then relaxes selection on structures that attract or handle pollinators. Selfing and reduced floral display often occur together, and both theory and experimental evolution show that mating-system change can interact with investment in attractive structures (Sakai 1995; Bodbyl Roels & Kelly 2011; Sicard et al. 2011; Gervasi & Schiestl 2017). Yet association and temporal order do not establish that assurance evolution is necessary for floral reduction.

This distinction matters especially on islands. Isolation can reduce the size and functional diversity of pollinator assemblages, while self-compatibility is over-represented among island plants (Traveset et al. 2016; Grossenbacher et al. 2017). At the same time, floral evolution on islands is not uniformly reductive: Pacific island comparisons show heterogeneous changes among lineages and islands, and functional composition can matter more than pollinator species richness alone (Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson 2020; Hiraiwa & Ushimaru 2017, 2024). A recurrent ecological pressure can therefore coexist with variable floral outcomes.

Two causal structures can generate the same cross-sectional association. In a serial pathway, pollinator limitation first changes the mating system, and the mating-system change then causes reduced attraction. Alternatively, pollinator limitation can directly reduce the reproductive return to attraction while also favouring reproductive assurance. In that case, assurance and attraction respond partly in parallel. Allowing assurance to evolve may still modify the magnitude of floral divergence without being required for its initial direction.

The second possibility creates a less obvious prediction. If attraction already has little reproductive value in a severely pollinator-limited environment, increasing assurance may have little additional attraction value left to displace there. The same assurance change could have a larger effect where maternal and paternal outcross returns remain substantial, even if that environment is not fully pollinated. Breeding-system evolution could then reduce the phenotypic difference between environments by pulling the better-pollinated population toward lower investment. Weak geographic divergence would not imply weak evolution.

We test these alternatives in one explicit reproductive model rather than by assigning independent response rules to attraction and selfing. Plants carry inherited matching, floral-investment and reproductive-assurance traits. Visitor functional types determine finite pollen transfer; reproduction includes maternal and paternal outcross contribution, prior or delayed selfing, inbreeding depression and allocation costs; Mendelian inheritance and finite recruitment determine realized evolutionary change. The model is mechanistic but intentionally not calibrated to a named island, species, geographic distance or natural evolutionary rate.

We ask three linked questions. First, does low visitor replenishment reduce floral investment when reproductive-assurance capacity cannot evolve? Second, when assurance can evolve, does it amplify or attenuate the investment difference between high- and low-replenishment environments? Third, which reproductive-return components explain any modification of that divergence? A separate temporal-order experiment asks which trait reaches a declared change first, but temporal sequence is not used as evidence of causal necessity.

# Materials and Methods

## Ecological and reproductive model

The model follows a finite plant population embedded in an externally supported visitor environment. Visitor functional types establish and disappear through time. Near and far treatments differ in successful visitor replenishment while plant carrying capacity and the remaining ecological rules are held fixed. The distance coordinate is a synthetic control on replenishment, not kilometres or island area.

Each diploid plant carries additive loci for a matching/access coordinate, floral investment and reproductive-assurance capacity. Investment affects visitor affinity and pollen export while incurring an ovule-allocation cost. Visitors differ in functional optimum, breadth and effectiveness. Finite pollen export and receipt generate a saturating outcross fertilization fraction. Pollen cannot return to the same individual.

Reproductive assurance follows one of four previously specified settings. The delayed-control setting has delayed selfing with no direct assurance cost or pollen discount. In that control, if unfilled ovules remain and selfed offspring have positive viability, a marginal increase in assurance has an automatic positive reproductive return. It therefore tests the resulting investment response rather than whether assurance can overcome its own direct trade-off. Prior selfing uses ovules before outcrossing. The pollen-discount setting reduces male outcross success as assurance increases. The assurance-cost setting imposes a direct allocation cost on assurance. Selfed offspring experience fixed inbreeding depression. These four settings were defined before the generality test and were not selected from its outcomes.

Outcrossed offspring receive maternal and paternal gametes; viable selfed offspring receive both genome halves from the same parent. Recruitment is finite and density regulated. Mutation acts independently on the three transmitted traits in the positive-mutation campaign. The model therefore distinguishes potential reproductive contribution, inherited composition and realized finite-population change.

## Upstream return to floral investment

Before analysing evolutionary trajectories, we measured the marginal reproductive return to investment at a fixed plant state. Total rare-mutant parental-genome contribution is
(W = F/2 + P/2 + S), where (F) is maternal outcross contribution, (P) paternal outcross contribution and (S) viable selfed contribution. The corrected invasion calculation changes the mutant trait while holding the resident pollen pool and recipient competition fixed; it is not a derivative that changes the whole resident population.

The investment gradient is decomposed additively into maternal outcross, paternal pollen-export, selfing-displacement and ovule-allocation-cost components. This accounting was independently checked against low-frequency numerical invasion. Fixed-state assays use visitor histories from the same ecological generator but contain no plant evolution.

## Prospectively frozen four-setting test

The primary generality experiment was frozen before its new outcomes were generated. It used 64 visitor-history seeds not used in either the original discovery cohort or the earlier independent confirmation, crossed with eight new nested demographic repeats. Each history was run for 1,000 reproductive updates under near and far visitor replenishment, fixed and evolving assurance, and all four reproductive settings. The positive-mutation campaign therefore contained 8,192 trajectories.

All plants began from the same 48-founder distribution for matching and investment. In both fixed and evolving modes, both assurance alleles were reset to 0.5, removing assurance standing variation from the comparison. In fixed mode, assurance remained 0.5 while matching and investment could inherit and mutate. In evolving mode, assurance also inherited and mutated. Fixed assurance therefore blocks assurance-capacity evolution but does not eliminate realized selfing: pollen shortage can still alter the number of selfed offspring at a constant capacity.

A separate 256-trajectory structural control used mutation probability zero. Fixed and evolving modes were required to produce exactly identical traces in every matched setting-history-repeat-arm cell. All 128 matched trace pairs passed.

The first primary estimand was the terminal far-minus-near investment contrast under fixed assurance. The second was the interaction
[
[(I_{far}-I_{near})_{evolving}-(I_{far}-I_{near})_{fixed}],
]
computed only on demographic repeats occupied at update 1,000 in all four matched cells. A reproductive setting passed the frozen rule only if the fixed far-minus-near contrast was negative with a 95% visitor-history bootstrap interval wholly below zero and the interaction was positive with its interval wholly above zero. The global rule required all four settings to pass; pooled effects could not rescue a failed setting.

Independent inference was at the visitor-history level. The eight demographic repeats were averaged within history and never counted as independent ecological replication. Trait endpoints after extinction were undefined rather than set to zero. The preregistered admissibility rule required at least 90% terminal occupancy per arm and at least 60 of 64 eligible histories.

## Post-confirmation localization of attenuation

After the four-setting gate had passed, we decomposed the confirmed interaction without rerunning or retuning the biological campaign. For each setting and history, we retained only repeats occupied in fixed-near, fixed-far, evolving-near and evolving-far. We calculated the within-arm effect of allowing assurance to evolve:
[
E_{arm}=Delta I_{evolving,arm}-Delta I_{fixed,arm}.
]
The interaction is exactly (E_{far}-E_{near}). We report the contribution (-E_{near}) as the near-side component and (E_{far}) as the far-side component. This is an algebraic localization of the interaction, not a natural direct/indirect mediation fraction.

We then asked whether the local selection mechanism was asymmetric between environments. At the fixed resident state matching = 0.5 and investment = 0.5, we changed assurance from 0.25 to 0.75 and recalculated the corrected rare-mutant investment gradient for all 64 matched visitor histories under near and far exposure. The primary presentation uses visitor snapshot 400; snapshots 0 and 200 are retained in the full result. We decomposed the high-minus-low assurance effect into the same maternal, paternal, selfing-displacement and allocation-cost terms. This diagnostic explains local selection only; finite trajectories additionally contain evolving states, genetic covariance, demographic sampling and feedback.

## Temporal-order and reproductive-consequence controls

Temporal order was tested separately in a prospectively frozen replication with 64 different new visitor histories and eight nested repeats. A trait crossed when its founder-relative change exceeded 0.05 for 20 consecutive updates; crossings within five updates were classified as near-simultaneous. This analysis is used only to assess sequence, not necessity.

A separate whole-population intervention compared pollen deficit and viable offspring production after changing investment at fixed plant traits. It tests whether lower fractional pollen deficit necessarily corresponds to greater viable reproduction.

## Statistical summaries and reproducibility

Intervals are percentile 95% bootstraps over visitor histories. All declared cells are reported regardless of direction. The four-setting campaign, arm decomposition and gradient-component diagnostic are stored as machine-readable results with source-design hashes and workflow provenance. The full campaign had terminal occupancy 1.0 in every arm and mode, so no primary contrast is generated by differential extinction or unmatched survivor sets.

# Results

## Visitor limitation reduces the reproductive return to attraction before plant evolution

At the same fixed plant state, low visitor replenishment sharply reduced the return to floral investment (Fig. 1). In the delayed-selfing setting at snapshot 400, the total investment-contribution derivative changed from +0.579 under near exposure to −0.700 under far exposure. The outcross contribution fell from +1.652 to +0.085, whereas the viable-selfed component partly offset rather than generated the decline. The intrinsic investment-cost coefficient was unchanged across environments. Thus visitor limitation can make reduced attraction favourable before any evolution of reproductive assurance.

## Assurance evolution is not required for investment decline

The prospective four-setting test passed its frozen non-necessity criterion in every setting (Fig. 2A). For the cost-free delayed-selfing control, direct assurance benefits are structurally positive whenever ovules remain unfilled; passing this control does not provide the same test of assurance trade-offs as the cost, prior-selfing and pollen-discount regimes. With assurance held fixed at 0.5, far-minus-near investment was −0.4413 [−0.4564, −0.4262] under delayed control, −0.3018 [−0.3172, −0.2866] under prior selfing, −0.3305 [−0.3461, −0.3151] with pollen discount and −0.4337 [−0.4501, −0.4171] with direct assurance cost. All four near/far arms retained terminal occupancy 1.0 and all 64 histories were eligible.

Reduced investment under low replenishment therefore did not require assurance-capacity evolution under any of the four declared reproductive rules. Because fixed capacity still permits realized selfing, this result excludes a requirement for assurance evolution, not all reproductive consequences of selfing.

## Assurance evolution consistently compresses environmental divergence

Allowing assurance to evolve reduced the magnitude of the near–far investment contrast in all four settings (Fig. 2B). These settings test a broader range of **investment responses** to assurance evolution, not four equally discriminating tests of whether assurance can invade: cost-free delayed selfing already favours assurance when ovules remain unfilled. Evolving-minus-fixed interactions were +0.1605 [0.1418, 0.1789] under delayed control, +0.2200 [0.2025, 0.2383] under prior selfing, +0.1915 [0.1762, 0.2069] with pollen discount and +0.1007 [0.0842, 0.1177] with assurance cost. All four settings therefore passed the preregistered attenuation criterion.

The arm decomposition showed that this attenuation was not primarily rescue of far-side investment (Fig. 2C). Relative to fixed assurance, allowing assurance to evolve changed near-side investment by −0.1253 [−0.1395, −0.1112], −0.1985 [−0.2137, −0.1838], −0.1699 [−0.1831, −0.1563] and −0.0789 [−0.0931, −0.0646] across delayed control, prior selfing, pollen discount and assurance cost. Far-side effects were much smaller and positive: +0.0352 [0.0233, 0.0471], +0.0215 [0.0116, 0.0315], +0.0216 [0.0110, 0.0327] and +0.0218 [0.0120, 0.0312]. Consequently, 78.1%, 90.2%, 88.7% and 78.3% of the interaction, respectively, was localized to additional investment decline in the high-replenishment arm.

## The direct selection effect is stronger where outcross returns remain available

The corrected rare-mutant diagnostic resolved the same environmental asymmetry at a fixed plant state (Fig. 2D). The near label denotes **higher visitor replenishment**, not pollen sufficiency. The independent fixed-plant snapshot-400 assay found mean raw deficit 0.3901 near versus 0.4948 far, and mean viable-seed deficit 0.5852 near versus 0.7423 far, in its delayed-timing assurance-cost setting across 64 visitor histories and fixed 48-plant states. Here the *raw* deficit is measured **after** autonomous selfing: with delayed assurance fixed at a=0.5, the source equation is d_raw=(1−a)(1−F/O), so mean outcross fertilization fractions F/O are approximately 0.2197 near and 0.0103 far. The 39% near deficit is NOT a claim that only 39% of ovules remain without outcross pollen. These fixed-state fractions are not terminal unfertilized-ovule fractions in evolved populations. Increasing assurance from 0.25 to 0.75 made the investment gradient more negative in both environments, but the effect was much stronger near than far. At snapshot 400, the near-minus-far assurance effect on the investment gradient was −0.3794 [−0.4376, −0.3170] under delayed control, −0.5231 [−0.6041, −0.4361] under prior selfing, −0.4661 [−0.5389, −0.3876] with pollen discount and −0.3794 [−0.4369, −0.3177] with assurance cost.

Maternal outcross and paternal pollen-export components supplied most of these differences. In delayed control and assurance cost, their near-minus-far contributions were −0.125 and −0.135, respectively; under prior selfing they were −0.214 and −0.235; with pollen discount they were −0.179 and −0.198. Selfing-displacement and allocation-cost components acted in the same direction, except that selfing displacement is structurally zero under prior selfing.

Under low replenishment, attraction-dependent outcross return was already small. Increasing assurance therefore removed relatively little additional attraction value. Under high replenishment, substantial maternal and paternal return remained, so the same assurance increase more strongly reduced selection for investment.

## Temporal order is setting-specific and does not establish necessity

The independent temporal-order replication recovered an assurance-first sequence in the delayed-selfing, direct-cost setting: 51 of 64 histories were assurance-first at the primary 0.05 threshold and 13 were near-simultaneous (proportion 0.797; 95% history-bootstrap interval 0.688–0.891). The same criterion did not generalize to prior selfing, where 30 of 64 histories were assurance-first and the interval spanned 0.5.

Thus assurance can change before investment, but this sequence is narrower than the four-setting non-necessity and attenuation results. Temporal precedence is neither universal nor required for the investment response.

## Pollen-deficit and viable-output responses are not equivalent

The reproductive-output intervention showed that apparent compensation in a fractional pollen-deficit metric need not correspond to higher viable offspring production (Fig. 3). In the delayed/far assay at snapshot 400, increasing investment from 0.25 to 0.75 at assurance 0.5 reduced fractional viable pollen deficit by 0.0104 while reducing viable maternal offspring by 15.72 per 48 plants. Reproductive assurance, pollen deficit and absolute viable production are therefore distinct quantities.

Finite-population and deterministic comparisons further delimit realization rather than the primary causal result (Fig. 4). They are retained to show that expected inherited response and finite genetic realization need not coincide exactly; they do not alter the four-setting intervention conclusion.

# Discussion

Our results separate three questions that are often combined in interpretations of pollinator-driven selfing syndromes: whether reproductive assurance is required for floral reduction, how assurance modifies divergence among environments, and when assurance changes relative to floral investment. The answers are different. Assurance evolution was not necessary for reduced investment under low visitor replenishment, but when assurance could evolve it consistently compressed the investment difference between environments. Assurance-first temporal order occurred in one reproductive regime but not another.

The non-necessity result changes the causal reading of the familiar selfing–flower association. Pollinator limitation can directly lower the marginal return to attraction because fewer effective pollen transfers remain available to gain, while allocation costs persist. Fixed assurance therefore does not prevent selection toward lower investment. This is consistent with long-standing theory that allocation to attractive structures depends on mating opportunities (Sakai 1995), but it rejects a stronger serial interpretation in which assurance evolution must occur before attraction can decline.

The attenuation result is more counterintuitive. Both source visitor regimes were incompletely pollinated, as independently illustrated by the fixed-plant pollen-saturation diagnostic. If selfing evolution simply intensified floral reduction under severe pollinator limitation, the far population should move farther from the near population. Instead, evolving assurance reduced the environmental contrast in all four reproductive settings. Most of the reduction came from additional investment decline in the high-replenishment environment, not rescue in the low-replenishment environment.

The local invasion analysis explains why this direction is plausible. Reproductive assurance changes the value of attraction through both female and male outcross function. Where pollination remains effective, investment still generates substantial maternal fertilization and paternal export returns. Increasing assurance reduces the marginal value of those returns, so selection on investment shifts strongly downward. Under severe visitor limitation, much of the same attraction-dependent return has already disappeared; there is less additional value for assurance to remove. Pollinator limitation therefore creates a floor in the attraction return available to lose.

A separate, **post-discovery** finite-ledger analysis clarifies an additional distinction without changing this preregistered result. In constructed Model 3 states, increasing one plant's investment can benefit the viable-seed production of other mothers even when the focal finite-parental log-gradient is negative. The resulting individual–group seed conflict depends on plant census and visitor context; it does not imply additional visitor recruitment. When total pollen *delivery* was matched across visitor compositions in 36 synthetic source states, group viable-seed differences almost disappeared, whereas expected paternal shares and local focal gradients could still vary among genetically distinct plants. These finite-focal gradients are not the rare-mutant invasion gradients used above, and neither group seed output nor this mismatch identifies long-term population persistence. A separate [mathematical supplement](CHAPTER2_POLLEN_FLOW_IDENTIFIABILITY_SUPPLEMENT_20261010.md) shows why total delivered pollen alone need not determine maternal seed yield and why even mother-specific pollen receipt fails to determine fathers when three or more plants can mate. These exploratory diagnostics are not independent ecological replication and are not part of the four-setting confirmation.

This produces an eco-evolutionary masking effect. Environmental divergence in pollination can generate divergent selection on attraction, yet breeding-system evolution can subsequently pull the better-pollinated population toward the low-investment phenotype. A small observed geographic difference can therefore arise not because selection or evolution was weak, but because evolution in one environment erased part of the contrast. Cross-sectional phenotype differences may underestimate the amount of evolutionary change occurring within populations.

A separate prospectively frozen longer-horizon **diagnostic of a different deterministic backbone and mutation-accessibility comparison** failed its stationarity criteria through 6,400 reproductive seasons. The high-standing/no-mutation response advantage over low-standing/central-mutation changed from 1.49-fold at 800 seasons to 0.91-fold at 1,600, and the latter overtook the former; this is a horizon-dependent genetic-input sensitivity, not a rerun of the four-setting near/far attenuation experiment. The same deterministic backbone became biologically hard to interpret because expected genotype-density mass fell below one plant in most histories. Therefore our 1,000-update evolutionary result is a **finite-horizon response**, not proof of an equilibrium syndrome or unlimited persistence of the attenuation interaction (separate design/result: `data/design/chapter2_long_horizon_stationarity_20261003.json`, `data/results/chapter2_long_horizon_stationarity_20261003.json`).

The result also clarifies why temporal sequence is an unreliable causal guide. Pollinator removal experiments have shown rapid evolution of autonomous selfing and floral traits, and sequential selfing-syndrome interpretations have explicit precedent (Bodbyl Roels & Kelly 2011; Busch et al. 2022). In our delayed/costly setting, assurance was indeed usually first. Yet investment decline survived when assurance evolution was blocked, and assurance-first timing did not generalize to prior selfing. A trait can change first because it responds more quickly or reaches an arbitrary measurement threshold sooner without being a necessary mediator of another trait's response.

Our result should not be read as evidence that selfing has no role in floral evolution. Fixed assurance is not zero selfing, and realized selfing can change with pollen receipt even when capacity is held constant. Nor does the arm decomposition estimate a natural direct/indirect mediation fraction. The finite trajectories include evolving trait distributions, linkage generated by selection, demographic sampling and ecological feedback. The fixed-resident gradient identifies a local mechanism that is consistent with the trajectory asymmetry, not a complete partition of long-term causation.

Generality is also bounded. The four reproductive settings deliberately span cost-free delayed selfing, prior selfing, pollen discount and direct assurance cost, and the preregistered generality result held across all four. In the cost-free delayed setting, assurance has a structurally positive direct reproductive return whenever receptive ovules remain unfilled and selfed offspring retain viability; that control does not establish an outcome independent of its assumptions. The assurance-cost setting is more discriminating and has the smallest compression interaction (+0.1007) and smallest near-side investment response (−0.0789). These results were obtained between two incompletely pollinated regimes; a fully pollen-saturated comparison has not been tested by the four-setting evolutionary campaign. A subsequent, explicitly post-discovery source-only feasibility screen multiplied the existing near-side pollen budget up to 16-fold but still failed a predeclared within-screen 90% outcross-fertilization criterion (mean 0.887; 48/64 histories meeting 0.90); this does not test evolutionary attenuation in saturated conditions (see [feasibility supplement](CHAPTER2_HIGH_REPLENISHMENT_POLLEN_LIMITATION_FEASIBILITY_20261010.md)). However, an independent broader parameter diagnostic found rare sign reversals in the local assurance-to-investment cross-effect under more extreme parameter combinations. We therefore claim robustness across the four baseline reproductive rules, not a universal theorem for all mating-system models.

The island interpretation is similarly structural rather than historical. Oceanic isolation can reduce pollinator network size and functional diversity, and self-compatibility is common on islands, but natural island lineages show heterogeneous floral responses (Traveset et al. 2016; Grossenbacher et al. 2017; Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson 2020; Hiraiwa & Ushimaru 2017, 2024). Our model identifies one reason why repeated ecological function need not produce proportional phenotypic divergence: mating-system evolution can change the mapping from pollination environment to floral investment. Testing this mechanism in nature requires longitudinal or transition-linked data that measure visitor replenishment, effective pollen transfer, mating route and inherited floral change on the same populations.

Finally, pollen limitation should not be treated as synonymous with fitness. Supplementation responses are context dependent, and resource reallocation can alter pollen-limitation estimates (Ashman et al. 2004; Knight et al. 2005, 2006). Our whole-population intervention gives a concrete model example: a smaller fractional deficit can coexist with fewer viable offspring because attraction costs, selfing and inbreeding depression remain. This distinction matters when using pollen-deficit metrics to infer the evolutionary value of floral traits.

The broader implication is that coevolving reproductive traits can obscure the phenotypic signature of environmental change. Selection generated by pollinator limitation can be real, repeatable and strong while the final geographic contrast becomes smaller because another trait changes the value of attraction differently across environments. Mechanistic tests of adaptation therefore need both intervention on candidate mediators and explicit separation of within-population change from between-environment divergence.

# Conclusion

Pollinator limitation reduced floral investment even when reproductive-assurance capacity could not evolve, and this non-necessity result held across four prospectively tested reproductive settings. Allowing assurance to evolve nevertheless compressed the near–far investment difference, mainly by driving additional investment decline where pollination remained effective. Corrected invasion gradients traced this asymmetry largely to maternal and paternal outcross returns that were still available to lose in the high-replenishment environment. Under these incompletely pollinated model regimes, breeding-system evolution can therefore mask part of the phenotypic signature of pollinator-driven divergent selection. Temporal order is a separate, setting-dependent result and should not be used as a proxy for causal necessity.
