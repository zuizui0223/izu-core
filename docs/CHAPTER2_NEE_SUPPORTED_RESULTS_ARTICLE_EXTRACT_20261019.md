# Recurrent reproductive assurance and contingent floral evolution in an island plant model

## Abstract

Island floras often display recurrent self-compatibility without a uniform floral phenotype, raising the question of why shared pollination constraints produce different evolutionary responses. We examined this problem in a finite diploid plant model that separates reproductive assurance, functional matching to visitors, and floral investment. Investment declined even when assurance expression was fixed, while allowing assurance to evolve modified investment contrasts between pollination regimes. Matched repeated-history analyses showed more concentrated assurance than investment endpoints across twelve model conditions, although fixation and inherited variation limit a deterministic interpretation. In a separate crossed founder-by-visitor design, visitor histories reduced held-out prediction error for matching change by 40.15%, versus 0.36% for investment change; neither value quantifies stochastic drift. Registered experiments showed that assurance evolution increased loss of alleles compatible with a founder-relative high-investment target by 8.33 percentage points under complete population survival, alongside a distinct 92.71-point policy-by-resource interaction in survival. Conditional transfers further distinguished retained target-compatible alleles from realized floral change. Thus reproductive buffering, visitor matching and floral investment can respond differently to pollination constraint, while population persistence does not guarantee genetic options for future floral evolution.

## Introduction

Island floras often show enriched self-compatibility without a universal change in floral size or display (Grossenbacher et al., 2017; Hetherington-Rauth and Johnson, 2020). This contrast motivates two reproductive problems: colonists must reproduce with available partners, and their descendants must respond as interactions change. Autonomous self-fertilization offers an immediate route through mate or pollen shortage. The same ecological argument suggests reduced investment in attracting pollinators when their service is scarce. This makes selfing and reduced floral display an intuitive pair. Yet the two responses perform different functions: one supplements fertilization, whereas the other changes access to an external reproductive partner. Their association raises a mechanistic question: when should reproductive traits evolve together, and when should their responses diverge?

The island setting makes this distinction especially useful. Selection during island colonization need not be equivalent to evolution after establishment: phylogenetic analyses of island *Coprosma* distinguish biased colonization from in situ morphological change (Ciarle, 2026). Colonists carry a sample of ancestral variation, so the traits favoured after arrival may differ from the changes genetically available to them. Visitor communities can also differ in both abundance and functional identity. A flower may become better matched to the remaining visitors without increasing its total investment in attraction. Conversely, a population may sustain seed production through selfing while losing variants required for a later floral response. Understanding reproductive evolution after colonization therefore requires connecting starting variation, the reproductive returns on individual traits, and the changes realized in finite populations.

These processes have strong precedents. Models link selfing to floral allocation and plant–pollinator demography (Lepers et al., 2014; Sakai, 1995), and reproductive assurance can rescue pollen-limited populations (Cheptou, 2019; Xu, 2023). Experiments document increased selfing and reduced genomic variation after pollinator exclusion (Busch et al., 2022). Floral reduction can accompany changing pollination and mating, with both selection and inbreeding contributing to the phenotype (Acoca-Pidolle et al., 2024; Tusuubira and Kelly, 2024). Together these studies motivate separating mechanisms that are often combined under the selfing syndrome. Self-compatibility describes the ability to self-fertilize; autonomous assurance describes a capacity to fill reproductive shortfalls; realized selfing records its use. Likewise, investment in attraction is an allocation decision whose expression may involve several floral traits.

Our central question follows a colonist through three stages: how does founding variation bound accessible change, which responses recur under island-like pollinator limitation, and which options remain in a subsequent environment? Our finite diploid model separates assurance A, investment I and visitor matching M. This permits interventions that hold assurance expression constant while allowing investment to evolve, or allow assurance evolution from the same initial expressed value. These comparisons distinguish whether assurance is required for investment decline from whether it modifies the decline. Archived repeated-history and crossed-source experiments then address realized variation, while registered policy comparisons measure persistence and retention of alleles compatible with a specified investment target.

Finally, we examine bounded transfers into new pollination environments. Establishment in such an environment need not require ancestral flowers: adaptation may involve a different matching trait or allocation. We therefore distinguish continued occupancy, ancestral-target accessibility and realized change. The resulting framework connects island establishment to later evolutionary opportunities through measurable population processes. Figure 1 summarizes the three traits and the five questions tested.

## Results

The five results distinguish intervention evidence, exploratory repeatability and conditional transfer (Fig. 1). Original Q1–Q4 outcomes, including null results, remain in the [extended evidence](CHAPTER2_FIVE_RESULTS_EXTENDED_EVIDENCE_20261021.md); [source-by-source recovery audit](CHAPTER2_R1_R5_SOURCE_RECOVERY_AUDIT_20261022.md) keeps contrary results and denominators explicit. ### R1 — Investment decline does not require assurance evolution

With expressed assurance fixed at A=0.5, floral investment declined under low visitor replenishment in four reproductive settings. Fixed assurance still permits selfing: the intervention blocks evolution of its capacity, not self-fertilization. In an independent eight-founder × eight-visitor study, fixed-A investment change from the actual founder was negative for all eight founder-averaged outcomes in each of four mating settings (descriptive across-cell means −0.217 to −0.287). This supports the non-necessity of A evolution beyond the original shared founder. A fixed-state investment gradient changed from +0.579 under higher visitor replenishment to −0.700 under lower replenishment, demonstrating context-dependent local returns rather than a guaranteed genetic response. Assurance and investment therefore need not evolve as successive steps of one syndrome (Fig. 2; [original Q1](CHAPTER2_UNIFIED_EVOLUTION_PERSISTENCE_MANUSCRIPT_20261010.md), [fresh founders](CHAPTER2_Q1_FRESH_FOUNDER_RESULTS_20261016.md)).

The distinction follows the reproductive accounting. Under delayed selfing, assurance acts on ovules left unfilled by outcross pollen. Investment instead affects both pollen receipt and export, while reducing the ovule budget through allocation costs. Its return can therefore fall as visitor service changes even when assurance remains constant. The fixed-A intervention exposes this route: a change in the external reproductive return is sufficient to produce investment decline within the tested model.

### R2 — Assurance evolution modifies environmental investment contrasts

Allowing A evolution compressed the near/far investment difference in all four original settings. This effect transported to three of four independent fresh-founder settings; the prospectively registered four-setting decision remained inconclusive because the direct-assurance-cost interaction interval included zero (Fig. 3). Within those fresh data, evolving-minus-fixed near-side I was negative in all four settings, but far-side I was also negative in two: compression is not universal protection of investment under severe limitation. Both original visitor regimes were pollen limited, so this is not a mainland-versus-island contrast. The interventions show that assurance can modify floral evolution without being required for its initiation.

The effect is compatible with altered reproductive returns, but does not identify its entire mechanism. Association-disruption extensions established neither assurance-specific hitchhiking nor its absence. In a separate exploratory 384-path baseline-pollen factorial, additional visitor decline produced negative I contrasts under strong baseline limitation but positive contrasts relative to maintained-activity controls under weak limitation; additional A response was also greater after weak limitation. Both inherited history and absolute pollen delivery differed, so this does not isolate a pure evolutionary-memory mechanism. These results separate a demonstrated policy effect from unresolved physiological and genetic mediation.

Assurance also changes this accounting. An additional outcrossed ovule has a smaller marginal viable-seed benefit when it would otherwise be partly compensated by selfing; investment costs also reduce ovules available to either reproductive route. The net effect includes paternal export as well as maternal receipt. This creates a mechanism through which evolving assurance can reduce the attraction advantage of the more favourable visitor regime and narrow the environmental contrast. The observed compression is the policy effect; this reproductive interpretation identifies a candidate route rather than allocating that effect among all possible mediators.

### R3 — Assurance endpoints concentrate, matching tracks visitor histories, investment remains contingent

An exploratory readback of matched repeated histories found lower within-context far-endpoint variance for A than I in all twelve setting-by-start summaries. For central starts, four repeats reached identical A endpoints in 66–111 of 128 histories, versus 7–20 for I. This endpoint concentration is not proof of consistently strong selection: fixation and standing-variation boundaries can contribute (Fig. 4a; [matched audit](CHAPTER2_MATCHED_AI_REPEATABILITY_20261019.md)). In central delayed-control starts, 103/128 contexts had unanimous A far-minus-near contrast signs but all 103 were zero contrasts. In a distinct B8 crossed cohort, 25,307/25,600 (98.9%) evolving-policy populations fixed genetically at the highest available A value (0.75), versus 5,145/25,600 (20.1%) under fixed expression, which still transmits latent A alleles. Across all A values, 25,582 and 15,664 populations fixed, respectively. The descriptive policy contrast supports a non-neutral direction in this model, but does not isolate selection from absorbing fixation or estimate their independent contributions ([source audit](CHAPTER2_A_DIRECTION_VS_FIXATION_EVIDENCE_20261022.md)).

In a separate 160-founder by 160-visitor-history cohort, held-out prediction of I change at B8 gained little from source identities. With evolving assurance, founder-only skill was −0.46%, history-only +0.36%, and additive skill −0.09%; the fixed-A comparison was similarly weak. Secondary history-only prediction of M change improved held-out squared error by 40.15% (Fig. 4b), versus +0.36% for history-only I change. These are improvements over a held-out intercept for unobserved pairings of **known** sources, not a fraction of flower morphology explained, an estimate for new founders or a fraction of I change caused by chance. Visitor histories varied matching optima under a shared count schedule; all visitors disappeared after update 35. Thus the result describes trait-specific predictability of historical effects, not universal environmental predictability.

The 97.622% combined residual for evolving-I change contains founder-by-history interaction and stochastic realization, which a single trajectory per crossed source cell cannot separate. It cannot be called a drift or chance fraction. Separate fixed-A repeated-history analyses found environmental structure in I, with eight-repeat reliability increasing from .621 to .825 at larger capacity. Density predictions likewise did not determine finite realization: two A-first density contexts produced that category in only 3/32 and 2/32 finite histories. These distinct designs show why weak main-effect prediction does not imply intrinsically unpredictable floral evolution. [Prediction analysis](CHAPTER2_FOUNDER_PREDICTABILITY_RESULTS_20261021.md); [density comparison](atom_comparison_20261011/ATOM_ABM_NUMERICAL_VERDICT_20261011.md).

The contrast between M and I is biologically interpretable within this design. Visitor identity supplies an external matching optimum, so histories differ directly in which M values encounter visitors effectively. I instead scales attraction and export while paying an ovule-allocation cost; its value depends on matching, assurance and the surrounding population. The model assigns no separate resource cost to M. These differences make stronger history-associated matching responses plausible, while leaving the quantitative cause of the skill contrast unresolved. For I, low predictive skill from source averages means that knowing a founder's typical outcome across other histories adds little for a particular held-out pairing. Genetic accessibility and realized response remain distinct properties.

### R4 — Assurance changes persistence and retention of a floral option

The preregistered 160-by-160 comparison matched initial expressed A at 0.5. At B8, all populations survived to census80; founder-relative high-I support was lost in 16,000/25,600 evolving-policy cells versus 13,868/25,600 fixed-policy cells. The difference was +8.33 percentage points (adjusted 97.5% interval 6.69–9.96). Under harsh resources, survival was 25,118/25,600 versus 1,385/25,600; the registered policy-by-resource survival interaction was +92.71 points (92.05–93.36; Fig. 5).

These are different resource conditions, not evidence that survival caused genetic erosion or was purchased through it. The genetic outcome concerns alleles compatible with one investment target, not general evolvability. All five prespecified descriptive high-I target tolerances retained positive B8 policy differences (8.33–8.60 points). A separate post-outcome all-start joint outcome, being alive with high-I support, differed by −8.33 points at B8 but +35.57 at B4.5; the latter largely reflects survival differences. Complete sealed endpoint readback reproduced the 25,600 paired cells. Exploratory founder/visitor-crossed 97.5% t intervals were −9.96 to −6.69 and +32.58 to +38.55 points, respectively; the between-budget policy contrast was +43.89 points (40.59 to 47.20). At B4.5 the marginal effect was positive for all 160 founders and all 160 visitor histories; 995 matched cells changed from a negative B8 policy effect to a positive B4.5 effect, versus six in the reverse direction. These are descriptive, post-outcome findings, not extra registered endpoints or independent sign tests. [Crossed-source audit](CHAPTER2_CROSSED_ACCESS_SIGN_REVERSAL_20261023.md). Exploratory polymorphism clocks also mix fixation and extinction under harsh resources. The registered intervention nevertheless shows that persistence and retention of a specified floral option are distinct consequences of assurance evolution. [Confirmation and raw audit](CHAPTER2_CONFIRM160_RESULTS_20261019.md).

The genetic endpoint asks whether surviving alleles still allow a descendant to reach its founder-relative investment target under closed inheritance. Losing that support removes one direction of future response even though reproduction can continue. The all-surviving B8 comparison makes this a within-population change in retained options rather than a consequence of which populations disappeared. Under harsher resources, demographic persistence becomes the dominant contrast. Reading these outcomes together reveals why a successful reproductive buffer and maintenance of a particular floral option require separate evaluation.

### R5 — Persistence, ancestral recovery and new-environment response diverge

A bounded exploratory transfer used 64 seed cohorts nested within 32 donors from four founders, four visitor histories and two assurance policies, with five destinations and 32 futures each. All 32 evolving-history cohorts entered fixed at A=0.75 and retained it in 5,120 futures. I was polymorphic in only 4/32 cohorts per policy; these supplied all I changes in visitor destinations. Yet 24/32 cohorts in each policy retained founder-relative high-I support, so support alone did not imply a capacity for further change (Fig. 6). Even without visitors, I increased in 27/1,024 fixed-history and 29/1,024 evolving-history future trajectories; such changes cannot be labelled adaptation to returning pollinators.

All evolving-history futures remained occupied, whereas fixed-history survival varied with destination. Restricted I response coexisted with changes in M and survivor-conditioned performance. Some cohorts already met the ancestral I target, and conditional performance gains do not establish an unconditional adaptation benefit. In a separate exploratory distant resident-mainland-like recipient, selected source markers remained in 2/8 fixed-history versus 4/8 evolving-history futures, whereas migrant genealogical descendants remained in 4/8 versus 6/8; resident community occupancy was 8/8 in both. Marker loss differs from lineage extinction, and neither proves successful independent mainland establishment or destination adaptation. These results distinguish persistence, ancestral recovery and response through other traits without proving a general recolonization advantage. [Transfer results](CHAPTER2_READAPTATION_RESULTS_20261019.md); [secondary-colonization audit](CHAPTER2_HITCHHIKING_SECONDARY_EXPANSION_REVIEW_20261020.md).

The transfer results also clarify the meaning of reversibility. A population already fixed at an ancestral-compatible I value may show no increase because it has little segregating variation and no restoration requirement. Another may lack the alleles needed for the same target. Meanwhile, M can change the fit to a novel visitor even when I remains constant. The biologically relevant comparison is therefore between the inherited state, the new reproductive environment and the response actually achieved, rather than between every descendant and one ancestral phenotype.

## Discussion

The study connects inherited starting conditions, selection on assurance and investment, finite realization, and subsequent persistence and genetic options. Three conclusions anchor this account: investment declined without evolving assurance; assurance evolution improved persistence under harsh resources; and, where every population survived, it increased loss of alleles compatible with a specified high-investment target. Together, these comparisons trace how the conditions for reproduction differ from the conditions for further floral change.

This changes how a syndrome can be interpreted mechanistically. Assurance evolution, reduced investment and loss of a future floral option need not measure progress along one evolutionary trajectory. Earlier work already recognizes multiple causes of floral reduction; our contribution is to separate necessity, modification and retained options through interventions. Holding assurance expression fixed tests whether its evolution is required for investment decline. Allowing assurance evolution tests how it modifies the response. Measuring survival and target-allele retention separately then tests whether demographic success preserves that option. Trait association or temporal order alone cannot substitute for these comparisons.


Arrival can sample alleles stochastically, yet the inherited state constrains subsequent evolution. Under closed transmission, missing alleles can make a target unreachable; available alleles do not guarantee its realization. Starting-state comparisons therefore reveal a conditional deterministic role for founder history: it restricts possible responses and changes threshold outcomes, without uniquely determining trajectories. This distinguishes a structural constraint on what can evolve from prediction of how much evolution occurs. The weak founder-only prediction of I change illustrates that distinction within the sampled founding distribution.



Common pollen shortage need not produce one floral trajectory. Assurance A can increase viable selfed seed production from unfertilized ovules, although mating order, costs and inbreeding depression modify the benefit. A endpoints concentrate, and a separate policy experiment frequently fixes the highest available A allele, without implying deterministic timing or order (A-first: 51/64 versus 30/64 across mating rules). Matching M responds to visitor optima, so histories predict M change better than I change in this design, where M has no separate allocation cost. I instead balances pollen receipt and export against allocation costs; its response combines selection, visitor history, inherited alleles and finite recruitment. Low predictive skill does not measure drift or chance, and M is not literal flower colour or architecture. These distinct mechanisms could link recurrent reproductive function with heterogeneous island floral traits, without establishing their historical causes.


Fixing expressed A still permits selfing. Investment decline under that policy demonstrates that evolving assurance is unnecessary, while the narrowing of environmental investment differences shows that assurance can nevertheless modify the response. Compression transported in three of four independently founded settings. Reproductive compensation can change the marginal return on investment; genetic associations can also transmit correlated changes. These are distinct candidate routes. The [association-disruption experiments](CHAPTER2_HITCHHIKING_SECONDARY_EXPANSION_REVIEW_20261020.md) establish neither assurance-specific hitchhiking nor its absence. Baseline-pollen interactions likewise include inherited history rather than isolating instantaneous deficit.


The [numerical comparison](atom_comparison_20261011/ATOM_ABM_NUMERICAL_VERDICT_20261011.md) uses discrete-update genotype density, not a full three-locus PDE or the finite model's exact stochastic expectation. All ten contexts passed adjacent-grid checks. In two contexts density predicted A-first, but only 3/32 and 2/32 finite histories realized that category despite complete survival; a smaller threshold changed the density classification. Thus favourable direction does not determine realized order. Starting variation, reproductive sampling and threshold crossing help define the distinction between a favourable direction and its finite realization; the comparison establishes that these levels of description can yield different sequences.


Selfing, allocation and demography are already linked theoretically (Lepers et al., 2014); reproductive rescue and genetic erosion also have precedents (Cheptou, 2019; Busch et al., 2022; Xu, 2023). Our initially expression-matched policy comparison separates demographic effects from retention of a directional floral option. At B8, complete survival excludes differential extinction as the explanation for increased support loss. At B4.5, assurance strongly improved survival. A post-outcome joint analysis shows opposite net effects on being alive with a specified high-I option between these two resource conditions; competing genetic-first closure and extinction-first are the relevant measured events. This does not establish a universal resource threshold, survival purchased through genetic loss, or genetic loss causing survival. Retained target support is not general evolvability.


A future mainland-like environment raises three questions: can descendants persist, regain ancestral traits, or adapt through different traits? These are separate outcomes. Late transfers distinguish support, polymorphism and realized response; some cohorts already met the ancestral target, and restricted I response can coexist with M-mediated improvement. Survivor-conditioned gains are not unconditional adaptation. Marker retention in small secondary-colonization experiments establishes no general assurance-mediated recolonization advantage. Closed-inheritance restrictions are not permanent irreversibility once mutation or immigration is allowed.

Islands may consequently change both which lineages persist and which responses remain available to their descendants. Comparative evidence of higher net diversification in self-incompatible Solanaceae (Goldberg et al., 2010) motivates connecting mating systems across population and lineage scales. Our results identify population-level quantities for that connection: persistence, trait-specific variation and access to subsequent response. A lineage can persist with restricted investment change yet retain another route to matching a new pollinator. Island evolution may therefore alter the directions available for later divergence, including after dispersal to another island or a mainland-like environment.

For empirical island comparisons, this interpretation suggests measuring investment allocation alongside floral dimensions, distinguishing visitor matching from total investment, and quantifying pollen limitation in both island and source populations. Repeated populations within comparable founding and visitor contexts would help distinguish environmental structure from within-context dispersion. Genetic variation for a specified floral response should be measured alongside persistence and realized selfing. These measurements would test whether the separations identified by the model also organize natural reproductive syndromes.


The model isolates processes relevant to island colonization using imposed visitor communities, fixed inbreeding depression and separately inherited traits. Its focal support, prediction and transfer cohorts exclude mutation and immigration, while some historical comparison experiments include mutation; I is allocation rather than flower size, and no expression plasticity is modelled. Founders come from restricted synthetic distributions. Prediction varies visitor identity under a common count schedule, with no independent repeat within each founder–history cell; interaction and stochastic residual remain confounded. M has no separate allocation cost, so the M/I skill contrast is design-dependent. Endpoint convergence does not separate selection from fixation, and assurance-specific hitchhiking remains unresolved. Original near/far regimes were both pollen limited. Transfer performance is survivor-conditioned, and natural recolonization, reproductive isolation and speciation were not measured. These boundaries preserve the distinction between observed policy effects and their candidate mechanisms.

Two questions organize these findings: which reproductive responses recur, and what can descendants change afterwards? Assurance endpoints were comparatively concentrated, visitor histories predicted matching better than investment in the crossed design, and floral investment retained context-dependent and within-history variation. Assurance evolution then changed persistence and retention of a specified floral option under distinct conditions. Connecting repeatability with retained options provides a framework for asking both what an established island lineage becomes and what it can evolve next.

### Historical model and inference ranks

The original Model 3 simulates finite diploid plants with separate additive visitor-matching, investment and autonomous-assurance loci. Exogenous functional visitors enter and disappear; encounter probabilities reflect floral affinity, matching optima, visitor effectiveness and pollen transfer. Maternal outcross, paternal export and viable autonomous-selfed offspring contribute to finite reproduction. Flowers incur allocation cost, and offspring alleles are inherited by Mendelian transmission with setting-specific mutation. The original Q1–Q4 and independent genotype-density experiments have separate registered inference units, horizons and source hashes; they are not pooled as one replicated island dataset. All original raw archives, readouts, negative/null outcomes and original EL-versioned figures remain unchanged.

The Q1 assurance-fixed policy sets actual expressed A=0.5; selfing remains possible. The 2,304-history structural-support experiment sets I mutation and seed immigration to zero. Its source restriction on **further decrease** is proved from nondecreasing minimum surviving I allele. The complementary **HIGH-I genetic support** restriction, evaluated in the read-only reanalysis and independently in a tiny later synthetic history, is that the maximum surviving I allele cannot increase under the same closed transmission rule. Thus a target I_star that exceeds this maximum is structurally unreachable in occupied descendants. The converse is not guaranteed by the theorem; selection, finite time and allelic pairing still matter.

### Model-derived functional insurance and evolutionary access are distinct

In source Model 3, cost-free delayed selfing acts as a buffer against failed outcross fertilization, not as a required replacement for outcrossing. For viable-self progeny factor v=1-delta, expressed assurance A, and outcross fertilization q, expected viable maternal seeds are Y=O[q+v*A*(1-q)]. At fixed inherited state, the fraction retained when q falls from one to zero is v*A, relative to that same state's original ovule budget. This is **functional pollen-failure tolerance**, not realized population persistence or long-term fitness. At fixed maternal ovule budget and assurance, the transmission of variation in annual outcross success to expected viable-seed production also follows Var(Y)=O²[1-(1-delta)A]² Var(q); insurance can attenuate the reproductive consequences of unreliable service. This is an algebraic source identity rather than an empirical estimate of stochastic extinction risk. With no direct assurance cost or pollen discount, an increase in A has nonnegative local return under delayed selfing; at perfect q=1 the return is neutral, not a genetic proof that A cannot decline.

Attraction I changes pollen receipt and paternal export while paying allocation cost. Under the closed inheritance rule, attaining a high-I target requires sufficient retained high-I allelic support. Selection and finite transmission affect whether and how rapidly that option is realized; favorable selection is not necessary for every realized increase. The mother's seed buffer does not guarantee retained support or subsequent response. Prior selfing, pollen discount and assurance resource cost can also make lowering A favorable after visitor return. Hence higher reproductive buffering may coexist with restricted recovery of high I, but the completed policy contrast below measures genetic support, not a causal tradeoff between buffering and realized recovery. The distinction is defined more precisely in the [source mechanism note](protocols/CHAPTER2_TWO_THRESHOLD_PRIVATE_SOCIAL_RECOVERY_SYNTHESIS_20261018.md).

### Crossed confirmation of support closure and persistence

The confirmation crossed 160 independent founder seeds and 160 independent visitor-history seeds. Historical assurance policies began with equal expressed A=0.5; the evolving arm retained A variation, whereas the fixed arm constrained expression while transmitting latent alleles. Within each crossed cell, the paired policy histories generated futures at resource budgets B8 and B4.5 through census 80. The primary genetic endpoint was first founder-relative high-I support closure before extinction at B8. The primary demographic endpoint was the evolving-minus-fixed survival contrast at B4.5 minus that contrast at B8. All allocated cells contribute to these policy contrasts; extinction is not coded as a zero-valued trait.

Registered inference used the fixed crossed-source pigeonhole variance convention and a t reference with 159 degrees of freedom. Two marginal 97.5% intervals implement the declared familywise alpha of 0.05; this convention has no universal finite-sample coverage guarantee. Target tolerances of 0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02 and 0.05 are reported without selecting a replacement primary target. The 10-percentage-point reference is a secondary resolution comparison, not an endorsed biological equivalence margin. Full source identities, raw denominators and registered comparisons are retained in the [confirmation report](CHAPTER2_CONFIRM160_RESULTS_20261019.md).
