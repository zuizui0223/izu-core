# Manuscript insertions from deep island-syndrome literature review

Updated: 2026-08-24

These paragraphs are editorial insertions for the Journal of Ecology manuscript. They do not alter any frozen result.

## Introduction insertion A — after the opening island-syndrome paragraph

The term **island syndrome** itself spans processes operating at different stages. Recent plant syntheses distinguish insular assembly rules—biased dispersal, colonization and establishment—from evolutionary changes occurring after colonization. This distinction is not cosmetic. In a recent review of New Zealand's outlying islands, 16 of 21 globally proposed plant island-syndrome components had been investigated, but only four were considered well supported, nine remained tentative and limited evidence suggested that three were not syndrome components in that region (Ciarle & Burns, 2025). Thus, the empirical literature supports recurrent island-associated patterns while also showing that their consistency and causal origin differ among traits.

## Introduction insertion B — sharpen the filtering / evolution distinction

The strongest reproductive example is Baker's law. Its modern scope is an **assembly prediction about the capacity for uniparental reproduction during colonization**, rather than a prediction of uniformly high realized selfing after establishment (Pannell et al., 2015; Pannell, 2015). Global comparative analyses support that filtering layer: self-compatibility is strongly enriched among island species (Grossenbacher et al., 2017), and island occurrence is jointly associated with breeding system, lifespan, pollination generalization and arrival opportunity (Zell et al., 2025). These assembly effects can therefore create a macroecological reproductive syndrome before any lineage-specific post-establishment evolution is considered.

## Introduction insertion C — show that post-colonization floral evolution is conditional

Floral morphology provides a contrasting case. A phylogenetically controlled comparison of 136 Pacific island-mainland sister contrasts found no universal reduction in island flower size, with direction varying among archipelagos and families (Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson, 2020). More recent analysis of 129 colonization events across ten Southwest Pacific archipelagos found size-dependent island-rule evolution in animal-pollinated flowers but gigantism in wind-pollinated flowers (Ciarle et al., 2025). These results shift the relevant question from whether flowers change in one island direction to **which starting states and functional contexts determine the direction of change**.

## Introduction insertion D — connect syndrome to functional pollination ecology

At the interaction level, oceanic island pollination networks are often smaller and topologically simplified, with lower interaction diversity and greater plant niche overlap, but network metrics do not change uniformly (Traveset et al., 2016). Generalization is recurrent but likewise incomplete: on Yongxing Island, plants were visited by more pollinator species than classical pollination syndromes predicted and floral traits explained only a minority of visitor preference (Wang et al., 2020). Most directly, the Izu-region comparison showed that loss of pollinator **functional diversity**, rather than species diversity itself, reduced community-wide trait matching and pollination success (Hiraiwa & Ushimaru, 2024). We therefore treat island-associated pollinator change as functional simplification or reorganization rather than as a simple decline in pollinator richness.

## Discussion replacement — opening of “Aggregate island syndromes can coexist with lineage-level branching”

The central ecological result is best interpreted against a three-layer view of the plant island syndrome. First, selective arrival and establishment generate **assembly syndromes**, such as enrichment of self-compatible or otherwise reproductively assured colonists. Second, established lineages can undergo **in-situ evolutionary change**, which comparative studies show may depend on mainland starting state, pollination mode, clade and archipelago. Third, established communities experience **ongoing interaction reorganization** as pollinator functional composition and partner structure change. The current model addresses this third, post-establishment layer while conditioning explicitly on differences among lineage starting states.

This decomposition resolves an apparent contradiction in the island literature. Strong aggregate syndromes do not require all established lineages to follow one evolutionary trajectory. Baker's law can bias which reproductive states arrive; subsequent selection can modify mating systems; flower-size evolution can change direction with starting size and pollination mode; and interaction networks can become simplified while retaining compensatory or specialist routes. The present results therefore reject a universal **post-establishment trajectory**, not the existence of recurrent island syndromes.

## Discussion insertion — link to starting-state literature

The replicated branch-generator result also gains independent conceptual support from recent island-floral studies. Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson (2020) rejected a universal directional flower-size syndrome across Pacific island-mainland contrasts, whereas Ciarle et al. (2025) recovered an island-rule pattern in animal-pollinated flowers in which the direction of island change depended on mainland starting size. Those studies do not validate the ABM's synthetic functional coordinate, but they independently show that **starting state can determine the direction of island-associated trait change**. The current model extends that conditionality from a comparative morphological pattern to a controlled response mechanism.

## Discussion insertion — network context

Likewise, the network literature argues against treating simplification as monotonic loss. Global comparisons show smaller and simpler oceanic pollination networks but metric-specific responses, while individual island communities can exhibit high generalization or super-generalist rewiring (Traveset et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020). Hiraiwa & Ushimaru (2024) further show that the functional composition of pollinators can matter more for trait matching and pollination than pollinator species richness itself. This literature supports the model's separation of a shared upstream functional perturbation from the local interaction context that determines whether its effects are buffered, redirected or worsened.
